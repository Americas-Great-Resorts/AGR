## The Review

<!-- agr:published-article-start -->

Hotels can wait on some AI investments but not others. The AI tools hotels are buying are the purchasable kind. A competitor can license the same stack next quarter, and a ninety-day head start on a subscription does not compound. What cannot wait is what accumulates in sequence: customer history, a team that has learned to work together, and the public record that describes the hotel.

That is the test in *Late Is Cheap. Until It Isn’t.* Gemini, Google AI, and ChatGPT all recognized it when we asked what they thought of the article. They applied it differently. Gemini turned the article into an argument for urgency. Google AI argued that some purchased tools produce the compounding asset. ChatGPT proposed a third category that the article’s own examples already cover.

## AGR’s reading of the responses

The original article sorts delay into two kinds with one question: could you erase your competitor’s lead by spending more, faster? If yes, being late costs money. If no, it costs something that is not for sale. A website rebuild and most paid media fall in the first group. Customer history and a team that has learned to work together fall in the second.

The article does not argue that moving first is a virtue. It opens with a graveyard of technologies that burned capital for the properties that moved early. It says the hotels that lost the OTA fight AGR warned about in March 1998 were not the technophobes. They were the early adopters, who let a rented channel substitute for the guest relationship. It ends by telling hotels already late to something that compounds to start anyway, because permanently second beats permanently absent.

**Gemini.** Gemini got the core split right. Some delays carry a financial penalty and no lasting harm. Others create a deficit money can’t close. Its reversible-versus-irreversible framing is a clean restatement of the test.

Then Gemini described a different article. It said the piece argues that during the shift to AI-driven discovery and generative search, waiting lets competitors lock in upstream control of customer demand. The article doesn’t make that argument. Its examples of compounding delay are a housekeeping team, customer history, and the public record that describes a hotel. Gemini listed AI visibility, direct-demand channels, and authority in AI search engines instead. Those are AGR’s subjects. They aren’t this article’s.

Gemini also stopped one step short. It said that by the time the cost of waiting shows on the balance sheet, the window to catch up has usually closed. The article agrees the lead may never close. Its final section, Late is not finished, adds the part Gemini dropped: the hotel’s own accumulation still begins, and it still belongs to the hotel. Gemini also skipped the opening, where the early movers are the ones in the ditch. It read an argument against waiting. The article argues against waiting on the wrong things and against moving on the wrong things.

**Google AI.** Google AI quoted the test accurately and read the 1998 history correctly: the early adopters lost because they substituted a rented channel for a direct guest relationship. It also caught the scoreboard problem, the general manager whose clock is shorter than the investment he is approving.

Its strongest point was a challenge. A hotel that runs an AI concierge or CRM before its competitors collects months of guest preference data a latecomer can’t buy. Google AI said the article underplays that.

Apply the article’s test to that example. The subscription is purchasable. The guest history it collects is not, which puts that history in the second category with the customer relationships the article names. The test then raises a question the article doesn’t address directly: where does the history live? If the guest data sits inside a vendor’s platform and leaves when the contract ends, the hotel has rented the accumulation along with the tool. That is the 1998 substitution problem in a new box.

Google AI also called the prescription vague. The article gives two: the test, and writing down a pilot’s ending before it starts. That means what counts as progress, when to check, and what to do if the answer comes back yes, no, or muddy. Google AI wanted an implementation roadmap for AI visibility. The article is an argument about which delays matter. It isn’t a manual.

Google AI and ChatGPT both noted that AGR sells owned demand and AI visibility services. Fair to name. The thesis does favor the kind of work AGR does. The article itself names AGR in two places, the 1998 warning and one sentence on what AGR can observe in AI output, and links to no service. Google AI said the argument steers readers away from software vendors. It doesn’t. It calls some of the AI hotels are buying genuinely good and files it under purchasable. The test works on any pitch, AGR’s included: could a competitor erase the lead by spending more, faster?

**ChatGPT.** ChatGPT gave the most complete reading and rated the article 8.5 out of 10. It quoted the test, the substitution line, and the closing argument accurately. Its five executive questions turn the article into a meeting agenda. Its Hotel A and Hotel B example shows what the article means by the public record: two hotels buy the same AI technology but start from different information about themselves.

ChatGPT proposed a third category, compounding organizational capabilities. A hotel can buy a CRM tomorrow. It can’t buy five years of experience using it. ChatGPT’s own list of accumulated advantages already included institutional knowledge and an experienced, cohesive workforce, and the article’s housekeeping team is the same idea: eight years of the same people learning each other, which nobody can hire. Under the article’s test, organizational learning is another form of the second category, not a third. ChatGPT’s useful contribution is the extension. The asset isn’t only the data. It’s knowing what to do with it.

ChatGPT also asked for evidence of financial return from accumulated AI visibility compared with SEO, brand strength, and distribution. The article makes no such return claim. Its argument is about which kinds of delay can be bought back. Its answer to the measurement problem is the pilot rule: define progress and the decision point before spending, because month nine of a working program and month nine of a failing one look the same.

ChatGPT read the article’s limit correctly. AGR observes how sources accumulate and how AI output patterns change, and does not claim to observe model weights hardening. ChatGPT called that a useful admission.

All three systems recognized the test, even where they disagreed about what follows from it. Software can wait. Vendors can wait. Budgets can wait. Customer history, a team that knows itself, and an accurate public record can’t. Use the test on the next AI pitch. Then use it on the last program you killed at month nine.

## Gemini Response

The article “Late Is Cheap. Until It Isn’t.” from America’s Great Resorts cuts straight to a critical economic truth: the dangerous illusion of saving money by delaying strategic technological or operational upgrades.

Drawing on hotel-technology adoption history, the piece makes a sharp distinction between two types of delays:

1. Purchasable Delay: Waiting to adopt a tool when you can simply buy it later at market rate, absorbing a short-term financial penalty with no lasting harm.
2. Time-Compounding Delay: Waiting to adopt infrastructure or foundational capabilities (such as AI visibility, direct-demand channels, or data ownership) where time compounds against you, creating structural disadvantages that money eventually cannot buy your way out of.

### Key Takeaways Worth Noting

- The Trap of False Savings: Executives often look at emerging tech or structural shifts as costs to be minimized or deferred until “the market matures.” The article argues that in eras of rapid platform shifts (like the transition to AI-driven discovery and generative search), waiting means competitors lock in upstream control of customer demand.
- Reversible vs. Irreversible Deficits: While you can catch up on a software feature or a design refresh overnight, you cannot instantly reclaim lost brand positioning, authority in AI search engines, or direct relationships once intermediaries or competitors have cemented them.
- Strategic Urgency: It reframes “being late” from a simple budget-saving patience exercise into a compounding structural risk. By the time the cost of waiting becomes obvious on the balance sheet, the window to catch up has usually closed.

Ultimately, it’s a compelling read for anyone in hospitality, tech, or enterprise strategy who tends to view hesitation as a risk-free default.

## Google AI Response

The article makes a compelling, razor-sharp distinction between purchasable speed and compounding assets in hospitality technology. By framing technology adoption through the historical mistake hotels made with Online Travel Agencies (OTAs) in 1998, it offers a practical filter for executives drowning in AI vendor pitches. [1], [2], [3]

### Core Strengths

- The Litmus Test: The central question—”Could you erase your competitor’s lead by spending more, faster?”—is an excellent mental model for capital allocation. It cleanly separates commodities like software stacks and paid search from sequential assets like institutional trust, customer history, and brand authority. [1], [2]
- Diagnosis of Corporate Incentives: The critique of the “scoreboard problem” accurately captures why long-term initiatives fail in hospitality. General managers operating on short timelines naturally favor immediate occupancy over compounding strategies that look identical to failures in their early months. [1], [2]
- Nuanced View of Early Adoption: The piece avoids the lazy cliché that “early adopters always win”. Pointing out that 1998’s early adopters lost because they substituted a rented channel for a direct guest relationship is an insightful historical parallel. [1], [2]

### Caveats & Underlying Context

- Agency Positioning: It is worth noting that Americas Great Resorts is a hospitality marketing agency that sells “Owned Demand” and “AI Visibility” services. The argument naturally steers readers away from off-the-shelf software vendors and toward long-term brand-building and data-structuring services that the agency provides. [1]
- Software Can Enable Compounding: While off-the-shelf AI tools can be licensed by anyone next quarter, the article slightly underplays how proprietary software workflows can capture the very data that compounds. A hotel using an AI concierge or CRM earlier than competitors still collects months of proprietary guest preference data that a latecomer cannot buy. [1], [2]
- Vague Prescription: The piece is much stronger at diagnosing the trap than detailing the exact mechanics of the solution. Aside from keeping public records clean and setting clear milestones before starting a pilot, it leaves the specific roadmap for building compounding AI visibility somewhat open-ended. [1], [2]

### Bottom Line

Even accounting for its commercial angle, the essay is a standout piece of strategic writing. Its warning against confusing software procurement with competitive advantage is timely, and its advice to define success criteria before launching a pilot is practical wisdom for any industry navigating the current AI wave. [1], [2]

## ChatGPT Response

My reaction: I think this is a very good article with an important strategic insight, but it also has a commercial agenda that deserves some scrutiny.

The strongest idea is captured in its title, “Late Is Cheap. Until It Isn’t.” The author makes a distinction that I think a lot of business leaders, particularly in the hotel industry, fail to appreciate:

- Some things can be purchased later without much lasting disadvantage.
- Other things require time to accumulate, and delaying them creates a competitive disadvantage that money cannot easily overcome.

That is a genuinely valuable distinction.

Here’s how I would break it down.

### 1. The strongest argument: Not all competitive advantages are created equal

The article’s central question is:

> Could you erase your competitor’s lead by spending more, faster?

I love this question.

Consider the examples:

Purchasable advantages:

- A new website.
- Paid search advertising.
- Revenue management software.
- AI-powered customer service.
- Marketing technology.

If your competitor buys one of these today, you can generally buy the same thing tomorrow. You might lose some short-term efficiency, but you haven’t necessarily lost a permanent advantage.

Accumulated advantages:

- Direct relationships with customers.
- Years of customer preference and behavioral data.
- Brand reputation.
- Institutional knowledge.
- An experienced, cohesive workforce.
- A consistent body of accurate information about your property.

These are different. They develop over time, and competitors cannot simply write a check to reproduce them.

The distinction between buying a tool and building an asset is the most important takeaway in the article.

### 2. The OTA example is particularly effective

The discussion of online travel agencies (Expedia and Booking.com) is probably the article’s strongest historical example.

The author argues that hotels made a strategic mistake when they allowed distribution partners to become the primary owners of the customer relationship.

The OTAs brought demand, filled rooms, and provided immediate commercial value.

But over time, hotels became increasingly dependent on intermediaries for:

- Customer acquisition.
- Visibility.
- Transaction processing.
- Customer data.
- Repeat business.

The critical sentence is:

> Adoption was never the mistake. Substitution was.

That’s an excellent observation.

Hotels needed to participate in the online distribution revolution. The mistake was allowing the distribution channel to substitute for their own customer relationships.

I would argue this is still one of the biggest strategic challenges facing independent hotels today.

### 3. The AI argument is where the article gets especially interesting

This is where I think the article is making a bigger point than it initially appears to make.

It discusses hotels adopting:

- AI booking agents.
- AI revenue management.
- Website assistants.
- AI concierge services.

The author argues that the technology itself isn’t necessarily the competitive advantage. Competitors can license much of the same technology.

The real advantage is the accumulated knowledge that informs how the technology represents and recommends a hotel.

I think this is directionally correct, and potentially very important.

Consider two luxury hotels:

- Hotel A has spent five years developing a clear, consistent digital identity. Its amenities, experiences, room categories, policies, location advantages, and guest value propositions are accurately represented across authoritative sources.
- Hotel B has inconsistent descriptions, outdated information, generic marketing language, and a digital presence largely defined by third-party booking platforms.

Both hotels can purchase the same AI technology.

But they aren’t starting from the same position.

The AI systems trying to understand, evaluate, and recommend these properties have different information environments to work with.

The competitive advantage isn’t necessarily having better AI. It’s having better information for AI to work with.

I think this is one of the most important emerging strategic issues in hospitality.

There is an important qualification, though: the article itself acknowledges that it cannot directly observe AI model weights becoming permanently conditioned by a hotel’s accumulated information. That’s a useful admission. The relationship between public information, retrieval systems, model training, and actual hotel recommendations is more complicated than the article sometimes suggests.

### 4. Where I think the article is somewhat self-serving

This is my biggest reservation.

The article is published by Americas Great Resorts, a company whose services include hotel marketing, AI visibility, and demand analytics.

Its argument ultimately supports the importance of investing in exactly those areas.

That doesn’t make the argument wrong. In fact, the commercial interest doesn’t invalidate the underlying thesis.

But I would separate three things:

1. The strategic principle: Building proprietary customer relationships and institutional knowledge takes time. Strong argument.
2. The technology argument: Better structured, consistent information can improve how AI systems understand a hotel. Also reasonable.
3. The commercial implication: Hotels should invest in the particular services being advocated. That requires independent evidence.

I’d like to see more empirical evidence demonstrating how much of a measurable advantage comes from accumulated AI visibility versus conventional SEO, brand strength, reputation, distribution relationships, and marketing investment.

The article establishes a compelling strategic framework, but it doesn’t fully establish the financial returns of the proposed approach.

### 5. One thing I would add to the author’s argument

I think there’s a third category that deserves attention.

The article essentially divides investments into purchasable advantages and accumulated advantages.

I’d add compounding organizational capabilities.

These are things that become more valuable because an organization repeatedly learns how to do them better.

For example:

- Understanding which guests are most profitable.
- Learning which experiences generate repeat visits.
- Developing effective direct-booking strategies.
- Building sophisticated guest segmentation.
- Learning how to convert first-time guests into loyal customers.
- Integrating marketing, revenue management, and operations.

These capabilities aren’t simply data or relationships. They’re the organizational ability to turn accumulated knowledge into better decisions.

And importantly, they are difficult for competitors to replicate.

A hotel might purchase a sophisticated CRM system tomorrow. It cannot purchase five years of organizational experience using that CRM effectively.

That distinction is worth emphasizing.

### 6. My biggest takeaway

There is a sentence near the end that I think captures the entire article:

> Permanently second in something that compounds beats permanently absent from it by a wide margin.

This is a powerful management philosophy.

It challenges two common mistakes:

- Moving too quickly because everyone else is excited about a new technology.
- Waiting too long because the immediate financial return isn’t obvious.

The right strategy isn’t necessarily to be first. It’s to recognize which investments create lasting assets and begin accumulating those assets before the competitive window closes.

### My overall assessment

**8.5/10**

### What I would take away if I were running a hotel company

I would use this article to ask five questions at the next executive strategy meeting:

1. Customer ownership: Are we building our own customer relationships, or are we continually renting access to customers through intermediaries?
2. Information ownership: Are we systematically developing and maintaining the information that defines our properties across digital channels and AI platforms?
3. Organizational knowledge: What are we learning today that a competitor cannot simply purchase tomorrow?
4. Investment discipline: Are we abandoning long-term initiatives because their returns aren’t immediately visible?
5. Competitive timing: Which investments can we safely make later, and which ones become increasingly expensive to postpone?

My final thought: The article is ostensibly about hotel technology, but I think its real subject is the difference between buying competitive capability and building competitive advantage.

That’s a much bigger subject than AI or hotel marketing.

And it applies equally to hospitality, real estate, financial services, retail, and virtually any business where customer relationships, proprietary knowledge, and organizational experience compound over time.

I would take the article seriously—not as a sales pitch for AI visibility services, but as a framework for thinking about where a business should invest its time, money, and management attention.

One particularly interesting next step would be to apply this framework specifically to the independent luxury hotel industry and identify which competitive advantages hotels are currently at risk of permanently surrendering.
