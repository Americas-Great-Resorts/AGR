Her assistant booked her elsewhere after saying the restaurant was gone and the hotel was reportedly closed for renovation. The restaurant had reopened and the renovation had ended.

## Gemini Response

That piece from *Americas Great Resorts*—titled **“The Hotel That Wasn’t There”**—is a clever, haunting piece of speculative fiction written in the classic *Twilight Zone* tradition.

It tells the story of Martin Hale, the meticulous general manager of a pristine, high-end Gulf Coast property called the Alder Point Inn. Despite offering world-class, bespoke hospitality, Martin watches his bookings quietly dry up. The culprit isn’t a failing website or bad customer service; it’s that **autonomous AI booking agents don’t know he exists.** The AI relies on an entrenched “shortlist” formed early in its memory from old digital footprints, leaving a brilliant, real hotel entirely out of the loop.

Here are a few core takeaways and thoughts on why this article is brilliant commentary on the current digital landscape:

### 1. The Shift From “Being Seen” to “Being Believed”

For decades, hospitality marketing was about SEO, pleasing human searchers, and driving traffic to a shiny homepage. The article brilliantly flips this paradigm: **being read is not the same as being believed.** AI models don’t browse the web like humans do; they ingest massive corpuses of text, historical reviews, third-party roundups, and outdated directories. If a hotel underwent a major rebrand or renovation, but the internet’s “ghosts”—old blog posts, dead links, outdated travel roundups—cemented a narrative years ago, the AI agent treats that ghost as the absolute truth.

### 2. The Danger of the “Consideration Set”

One of the most profound concepts in the story is how AI booking agents operate. They don’t mathematically scan every hotel on a coastline from scratch every time a user says, *“Book me a nice quiet weekend.”* Instead, they rely on pre-existing semantic clusters and shortlists.If a hotel isn’t already deeply embedded in the model’s foundational training data as a top-tier recommendation, it never even enters the race. It creates a brutal winner-take-all ecosystem where traditional excellence (like Martin knowing which guest likes their window cracked an inch) is completely invisible to algorithmic gatekeepers.

### 3. A Wake-Up Call Wrapped in Fiction

While framed as a Twilight Zone-style parable, the article serves as a sharp wake-up call for the hospitality, real estate, and service industries. As consumer behavior shifts rapidly from manual browsing on OTA (Online Travel Agency) sites to conversational AI booking assistants, businesses can no longer rely on physical curb appeal alone. They have to manage their **AI digital footprint**—ensuring that the data feeding large language models is accurate, synchronized, and robust enough to make it onto the machine’s shortlist.

Ultimately, it’s a sobering reminder that as automation claims a larger slice of consumer decision-making, the scariest thing that can happen to a business isn’t getting a bad review—it’s becoming a ghost in the machine.

## Google AI Response

“The Hotel That Wasn’t There” is a brilliantly written, cautionary piece of modern marketing allegory. It captures a profound and terrifying shift in commerce: **the transition from Search Engine Optimization (SEO) to AI Engine Optimization (or “Knowledge Engine Optimization” / KEO).**

The article argues that having a great product, loyal customers, and a perfect website no longer guarantees a business will survive if AI agents do not “believe” it exists.

Here is a breakdown of my thoughts on the core concepts, the technical accuracy behind the story, and the broader implications for the future.

---

**1. The Core Shift: Discovery vs. Curation**

In the traditional internet era, users used search engines to *discover*—they looked at a list of links, did the research, and made a choice. In the AI era, agents *curate and decide*. As the article highlights through the character of Ruth, a consumer may completely bypass a lifetime of personal brand loyalty because their AI assistant matter-of-factly tells them a business is closed or irrelevant. The friction of arguing with an AI assistant is often higher than simply accepting its recommendation.

**2. Technical Accuracy: The Mechanics of “Belief”**

The author beautifully translates complex AI concepts into a narrative arc, and the underlying computer science is entirely accurate:

- **Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):** The article correctly notes that AI agents don’t wait for explicit commands; they observe behavior (sleep patterns, email tone, calendar openings) to infer user desires and make decisions.
- **Solomonoff’s Induction / Ockham’s Razor:** LLMs and data aggregators inherently favor clean, structurally consistent data. If a hotel has one clean digital footprint (like the Saltwell), the model processes it with high confidence. If it has fragmented, messy, historical data across old directories (like the Alder Point), the model down-ranks it due to uncertainty.
- **The Minority Report Effect:** This is the most dangerous aspect of LLMs. When Martin updates his website, the AI *reads* it, but it treats the single live website as a “dissenting opinion” against a massive historical backdrop of old internet data saying the hotel is closed or under a different name. The AI defaults to the algorithmic majority.

**3. The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy**

The most haunting takeaway of the piece is that **AI doesn’t just predict reality; it creates it**. By redirecting traffic away from the Alder Point based on a false premise (that it was closed), the AI starved the business of revenue, forcing the restaurant to close and occupancy to plummet. Ultimately, the AI’s hallucination or outdated memory *became true*.

---

**The Big Picture Dilemma**

While this article is explicitly framed as a pitch for a hotel “AI Visibility Report,” the underlying warning applies to **every business on Earth**. We are entering an era where data cleanliness, authoritative digital footprints, and actively managing how AI models perceive your brand are more important than traditional advertising. If the machines don’t know you exist, or if they find your digital history too messy to trust, you effectively vanish from the consumer marketplace.

## ChatGPT Response

My honest take: This is a very good article, and it identifies a problem that the hotel industry should take seriously. But I think its most important argument goes even deeper than AI visibility.

The central idea is that a business can be perfectly real, operational, well-reviewed, and even have an excellent website, yet effectively disappear from the marketplace if AI systems don’t recognize it, trust its information, or include it in their recommendations.

I think that’s a legitimate and potentially enormous issue.

The line that really captures the article’s message is:

> “Being read is not the same as being believed.”

That’s an exceptionally important distinction.

Let me explain what I think the author gets right, where I think the article stretches things, and what I believe the implications are.

### 1. The author is identifying a fundamental shift in how customers find businesses

Historically, hotels competed for visibility through:

- Google search rankings.
- Online travel agencies (Expedia, Booking.com, etc.).
- Reviews and reputation.
- Advertising.
- Their own websites and email marketing.

The customer generally initiated the search, evaluated options, and made a decision.

AI agents introduce a different dynamic.

Instead of asking, “What hotels are available in this area?” a customer might say:

“Find me a beachfront hotel for four nights. I like quiet properties, excellent restaurants, and ocean views. Book something that fits my preferences.”

The AI increasingly becomes the researcher, evaluator, recommendation engine, and potentially the booking intermediary.

That changes the competitive landscape.

A hotel doesn’t just need to be attractive to the customer anymore. It needs to be understandable, discoverable, and credible to the systems representing that customer.

That’s a substantial change in the economics of customer acquisition.

### 2. The most important insight is the concept of the AI shortlist

I think this is the strongest part of the article.

The author argues that the AI doesn’t necessarily evaluate every hotel when making a recommendation. Instead, it starts with a collection of properties it already knows or considers credible.

That creates a potentially dangerous situation.

Imagine two hotels:

- Hotel A has been operating for 30 years, has outstanding service, excellent reviews, and loyal customers, but inconsistent information across the internet.
- Hotel B has been operating for five years, has a strong digital footprint, consistent business information, and a well-established online reputation.

Hotel B might consistently make the AI’s consideration set, while Hotel A gets overlooked.

Even if Hotel A is objectively better.

This is important because you cannot win a recommendation if you never make the consideration set.

And unlike traditional search rankings, AI recommendations may not give a business an obvious position to monitor or improve.

### 3. Where I think the article is overstating the case

This is where I would be a little more cautious.

The article blends several different technologies and behaviors together:

1. AI systems that retrieve information from the internet.
2. AI assistants that recommend hotels based on a user’s request.
3. Autonomous agents that compare prices and availability.
4. Booking platforms that actually execute transactions.

These are related, but they aren’t the same thing.

For example, ChatGPT recommending a hotel doesn’t necessarily mean it has access to that hotel’s live inventory or can book a room directly.

Likewise, a hotel not appearing in one AI recommendation doesn’t necessarily mean the hotel is excluded from some permanent, invisible shortlist.

The article presents the fictional Martin Hale’s experience as a coherent future scenario. It is compelling storytelling, but it isn’t evidence that this exact process is already happening.

There is, however, some interesting research supporting the underlying concern.

A 2026 study published in Information Technology & Tourism, titled “Whose Hotel Does the AI Recommend?”, examined how different AI models select hotels. It found that ratings, pricing, and even the position of a hotel in a presented list could influence recommendations. Importantly, different models didn’t necessarily behave the same way.

### 4. The biggest danger is that AI can perpetuate outdated information

This is the part I find especially interesting.

Consider what happens when a hotel:

- Changes its name.
- Changes ownership.
- Renovates its property.
- Reopens a restaurant.
- Changes its positioning from a family resort to a luxury boutique property.
- Repositions itself in the marketplace.

Its website might be completely updated, but older descriptions can continue circulating through directories, travel publications, review platforms, and other websites.

AI systems can encounter conflicting information.

And here’s the important distinction:

The newest information isn’t necessarily the information an AI system will use.

A system might encounter an older description more frequently, or find it on sources it considers more useful for a particular question.

The article’s example of the restaurant being incorrectly described as closed is a good illustration of how a relatively small factual error can have serious commercial consequences.

This is not just an SEO problem. It’s a reputation and information-management problem.

### 5. I would be careful about the article’s commercial angle

There’s something worth recognizing about the source.

Americas Great Resorts is a hospitality marketing company, and the article concludes by inviting hotels to purchase an AI Visibility Report.

Americas Great Resorts

That doesn’t invalidate the argument.

In fact, companies that recognize an emerging problem early can be valuable resources.

But I would separate three things:

- The problem: AI systems can misrepresent businesses or fail to recommend them.
- The proposed solution: Manage the information environment that AI systems encounter.
- The commercial proposition: Pay a specialist to measure and improve your AI visibility.

The first is credible. The second is worth exploring. The third needs to be evaluated with evidence.

I would want to see independent measurements demonstrating that a particular optimization strategy actually increases recommendations and, ultimately, produces incremental bookings.

I wouldn’t spend significant money simply because someone promises to make a hotel more visible to AI.

### 6. What I think hotels should actually do about this

I would approach this as a new discipline in hotel marketing, rather than simply another form of SEO.

I’d break it into five areas:

| Area | What needs to happen |
| --- | --- |
| Identity | Ensure the hotel’s name, location, ownership, and positioning are consistent everywhere. |
| Accuracy | Correct outdated information about amenities, restaurants, renovations, and operations. |
| Authority | Develop credible third-party references through travel publications, respected reviewers, and industry sources. |
| Differentiation | Make it easy for AI to understand what makes the hotel genuinely different. |
| Measurement | Regularly test what AI systems say about the property and whether they recommend it. |

I would also establish a recurring testing process.

For example, ask several AI platforms:

- What are the best luxury beachfront hotels in this destination?
- Which hotels would you recommend for a couple seeking a quiet getaway?
- Which properties have the best restaurants?
- What are the most distinctive independent hotels in this market?
- Tell me about Hotel X, including its amenities, restaurant, and current operating status.

Then repeat those questions over time.

Don’t just record whether the hotel appears. Record:

- Whether the information is accurate.
- Which competing hotels are recommended.
- Which sources support the recommendations.
- Whether the hotel is consistently included.
- Whether the AI understands its unique selling propositions.

And most importantly, connect those findings to actual website traffic, inquiries, and bookings.

An AI visibility score without a commercial outcome is just another marketing metric.

### 7. My biggest takeaway: This is about who controls the relationship with the customer

There’s a larger issue here that I think the article only touches on.

For decades, hotels have been trying to reduce their dependence on intermediaries.

They want direct bookings, direct relationships with guests, control over their brand positioning, and ownership of their customer relationships.

Now we may be entering a period in which AI becomes another intermediary.

Imagine a future where:

- A guest doesn’t visit Expedia.
- A guest doesn’t visit Booking.com.
- A guest doesn’t visit the hotel’s website.
- An AI assistant makes the recommendation.
- An AI agent compares the options.
- An AI agent completes the booking.

The hotel may receive the reservation without ever having an opportunity to communicate its brand story directly to the guest.

That has enormous implications for customer acquisition costs, distribution strategy, brand differentiation, and customer loyalty.

And there’s another concern: If AI systems repeatedly recommend the same handful of properties, those properties accumulate more reviews, mentions, and visibility, potentially reinforcing their advantage.

The hotel that starts ahead could become increasingly difficult to displace.

### My overall assessment

The article

Important argument

#### 8.5/10

The storytelling is excellent, the underlying business concern is legitimate, and the timing is important.

What I agree with

- AI discovery is becoming a meaningful distribution issue.
- Inconsistent information can damage a hotel’s digital representation.
- Being indexed or crawled does not guarantee being recommended.
- Hotels need to understand how AI describes and evaluates them.
  What I would challenge
- The article makes hypothetical agent behavior sound more established than it is.
- It doesn’t demonstrate that the fictional occupancy decline would actually result from AI visibility.
- It doesn’t establish that its proposed commercial solution produces additional bookings.

One final thought.

I think the hotel industry may be approaching a transition comparable to the arrival of online travel agencies and, later, Google Hotel Search.

But this time, the change could be even more fundamental.

Previously, hotels competed to appear in search results.

Now they may increasingly compete to be understood, trusted, and selected by artificial intelligence.

Those are three different challenges.

And I suspect the hotels that recognize that distinction early will have a meaningful competitive advantage.

I’d take this article seriously—not as a prediction that every hotel will soon be controlled by AI agents, but as an early warning about a new battleground for hotel distribution.
