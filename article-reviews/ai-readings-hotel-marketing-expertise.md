**AGR’s reading of the responses**

The article uses Richard Feynman’s story about knowing a bird’s name to draw a distinction between vocabulary and mechanism. Its test goes further. A consultant should be able to explain how the work produces a result, when it fails, and what evidence would prove the explanation wrong.

**Gemini.** Gemini focused on diagnosis and repair. It read the article as a challenge to identify the mechanism behind a hotel’s problem and explain how to reverse it. Its response left two parts of AGR’s test unaddressed. When would the approach fail? What result would prove the explanation wrong?

**Google AI.** Google AI preserved the article’s question about why a strategy works and when it fails. It applied the bird analogy to itself, describing AI as fluent in industry vocabulary. It also called superficial consultants imposters. AGR’s opening describes someone with real credentials who knew the vocabulary and still could not solve the problem.

**ChatGPT.** ChatGPT asked how AGR would establish what caused a hotel’s results and whether the method would work at another property. It asked for a baseline, what else changed during the period, and how much of the result was incremental. It distinguished the standard the article sets from the evidence it presents on the page. One sentence describes the article’s target as experts who explain marketing concepts. AGR’s criticism is aimed at consultants whose offer stops at naming the problem.

ChatGPT’s response focuses on the article itself and leaves out the case study linked from the word “builds.” That study compares the same six months year over year at a flat $750 average daily rate. It reports 627 direct room nights matched to campaign recipients whose email addresses were absent from the hotel’s supplied prior-guest file. The matchback identifies bookings made by recipients. ## Gemini Response

This article, titled **“You Can Name Him. Can You Explain Him?”** from *Americas Great Resorts* (authored by Andrew Paul), makes a sharp, compelling point about the nature of expertise—particularly in the marketing industry.

Here is a breakdown of the core themes, strengths, and underlying perspective of the piece:

### 1. The Core Premise: Naming vs. Explaining

The article hinges on a classic philosophical distinction popularized by physicist Richard Feynman: **knowing the name of something versus actually understanding how it works.**

- **The “Naming” Expert:** Many consultants and marketing agencies sound sophisticated because they can effortlessly label your symptoms. They use high-level industry jargon—identifying issues like “low conversion velocity,” “funnel friction,” or “poor brand equity.”
- **The “Explaining” Expert:** True depth begins when someone can move past the diagnosis and lay bare the *mechanism*—the underlying behavioral, structural, or algorithmic cause of *why* something is happening and precisely how to fix it from the ground up.

### 2. Application to Modern Marketing (and AI Visibility)

Given that the publication specializes in luxury hospitality, travel, and AI search positioning, this critique carries a lot of weight in the current digital landscape:

- **The Illusion of Competence:** In fields like Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) and digital marketing, it is easy for agencies to hide behind buzzwords. They can tell you that your luxury hotel or property is “losing AI visibility,” but they often fail to explain the mechanical architecture of *how* LLMs (like ChatGPT or Gemini) fetch, weigh, and synthesize entities.
- **Shifting from Rhetoric to Frameworks:** The piece implicitly argues that modern executives need to stop hiring vendors who are merely fluent in industry buzzwords and instead look for practitioners who operate with deep, structural mechanics.

### Final Thoughts

It’s a brief, punchy read, but it serves as an effective diagnostic tool for leadership teams. It acts as a warning against superficial consultants: **if a marketer can label your problem eloquently, but cannot map out the exact mechanical chain reaction that caused it and how to reverse it, they don’t actually understand it.**

## Google AI Response

This article presents a sharp, highly relevant critique of contemporary consultancy, particularly within the hospitality marketing sector. **Its core argument is that artificial intelligence has commoditized the mere vocabulary of expertise (“naming”), shifting all actual human value to the mastery of underlying mechanics and failure states (“explaining”)**. [1]

By using Richard Feynman’s famous bird analogy, the author effectively highlights a major pain point for businesses: hiring fluent talkers who understand the terminology but cannot execute a strategy. [1]

**1. The Distinction: Naming vs. Explaining**

The article breaks down the two types of consultants businesses encounter:

- **The Namer:** Relies on recall, credentials, and buzzwords (e.g., “owned demand,” “AI visibility,” “full-funnel”). They can identify a problem and present clean decks, but their understanding stops at the surface level. [1, 2]
- **The Explainer:** Focuses on mechanism and derivation. They can take the system apart, explain how demand forms, build infrastructure from scratch, and pinpoint the exact conditions under which a strategy will fail. [1]

**2. The AI Disruption of “Fluency”**

The author’s most compelling point is that **generative AI has fundamentally destroyed the market value of the “Namer.”** Because large language models can generate flawless, highly confident marketing prose and recall every textbook definition instantly, human professionals can no longer charge a premium just for sounding like an expert. If a consultant’s primary output is a polished presentation of standard industry concepts, they are already obsolete. [1, 2]

**3. The Litmus Test: Falsifiability and Failure**

The article offers highly practical advice for vetting partners by introducing a simple question: **“Why, and when does it fail?”**.

- True experts embrace falsifiability; they know no strategy works universally and can outline the parameters of failure.
- Imposters or surface-level thinkers will falter because their knowledge is based on static recitation rather than dynamic mechanics.

**An AI’s Perspective on the Piece**

The article is entirely correct. As an AI, I am trained on vast repositories of industry language, meaning I can “name the bird” with total fluency. However, the true test of utility—both for an AI tool and a human consultant—is moving past recitation to uncover the explicit trade-offs, hidden dependencies, and precise failure points of a given approach.

The piece serves as an excellent wake-up call for agencies and a practical rubric for executives trying to protect their margins from empty eloquence.

## ChatGPT Response

My overall reaction: I think this is a very good article with an important message, but it is also a carefully constructed piece of marketing in its own right. And that distinction is worth examining.

The central argument is compelling: there is a tremendous difference between someone who knows the terminology of a discipline and someone who genuinely understands how to make things work.

The author expresses this through the distinction between naming and explaining.

I agree with that distinction almost entirely.

Here is how I would break it down.

### 1. The strongest idea: knowing the vocabulary isn’t knowing the business

The article makes a particularly important point:

Someone can sit in a room and talk fluently about:

- AI visibility.
- Owned demand.
- Attribution.
- Full-funnel marketing.
- Brand equity.
- OTA dependency.

They can sound incredibly knowledgeable without actually knowing how to solve the underlying problems.

This is especially relevant in hospitality marketing.

For example, telling a hotel owner that they need to reduce their dependence on Expedia or Booking.com is relatively easy.

Explaining how to accomplish that is an entirely different matter.

You need to understand:

- Where the hotel’s demand actually originates.
- Why customers choose an OTA instead of booking directly.
- What motivates customers to book directly.
- How distribution economics affect profitability.
- How to generate incremental direct demand.
- How to measure whether marketing is actually producing incremental revenue.

Those are operational and commercial questions, not vocabulary questions.

The article is absolutely right that the person who can explain the mechanics is much more valuable than the person who can simply describe the problem.

### 2. The Feynman analogy is excellent

The reference to physicist Richard Feynman’s story about naming birds is probably the strongest intellectual component of the article.

Knowing what something is called doesn’t mean you understand what it does.

I would take that analogy one step further.

A genuinely knowledgeable marketing professional should be able to explain a system at three levels:

1. What: What are we trying to accomplish?
2. How: What mechanisms actually produce the desired outcome?
3. Why: Why should those mechanisms work, under what circumstances, and what evidence would demonstrate that they don’t?

The third level is particularly important.

Someone who truly understands a marketing discipline should be able to identify the assumptions behind their recommendations and acknowledge when those assumptions are wrong.

That is a much better test of expertise than a presentation full of impressive terminology.

### 3. The AI argument is particularly interesting

The article makes a provocative assertion: AI has effectively eliminated the value of experts whose primary skill is explaining marketing concepts.

I think there is considerable truth in this.

AI can now:

- Produce sophisticated marketing strategies.
- Explain complicated marketing terminology.
- Generate competitive analyses.
- Develop campaign recommendations.
- Produce polished presentations.
- Articulate problems and potential solutions.

Consequently, simply knowing more marketing terminology is no longer a meaningful competitive advantage.

But I would make an important distinction.

AI can explain a mechanism without necessarily being able to validate that the mechanism works in a particular business.

For example, AI can explain how a hotel’s website, structured data, reviews, brand authority, and third-party references might influence its visibility in AI-generated travel recommendations.

But that doesn’t automatically mean it can determine which factors are actually limiting a particular hotel’s visibility, implement the necessary changes, and demonstrate measurable commercial improvement.

That requires data, experimentation, implementation, and accountability.

And that’s where experienced practitioners still have substantial value.

### 4. Where I think the article is less convincing

This is where I would challenge the author a little.

The article creates a fairly sharp division:

- The expert who names things.
- The expert who understands and builds things.

That’s a powerful rhetorical device, but real-world expertise isn’t always that binary.

There are people who are excellent strategists but rely on specialized teams to implement their strategies. There are also excellent technicians who can build sophisticated systems but don’t necessarily understand the commercial objectives of the business.

Both can be valuable.

In my opinion, the best marketing experts combine three capabilities:

- Strategic understanding: Knowing what needs to happen and why.
- Technical competence: Knowing how to make it happen.
- Commercial accountability: Knowing whether the work produces a meaningful business result.

I would also caution against assuming that someone who can explain something exceptionally well necessarily understands it exceptionally well.

A person can construct a convincing explanation that is still wrong.

The ultimate test is whether their explanation survives contact with reality.

### 5. There is an interesting irony in this article

I think this is perhaps the most interesting aspect of the entire piece.

The article criticizes marketing professionals who are good at describing problems but cannot actually solve them.

Yet the article itself is essentially a marketing message designed to establish the author’s credibility.

Look at its construction:

1. It identifies a problem that prospective clients recognize.
2. It describes the frustration of having hired ineffective experts.
3. It introduces a memorable intellectual framework.
4. It explains why AI makes superficial expertise obsolete.
5. It positions the author as someone who understands mechanisms rather than merely terminology.

That’s sophisticated positioning.

And there is nothing inherently wrong with that.

However, the article creates an obligation for its author.

If someone argues that expertise must be demonstrated through mechanisms, implementation, and measurable results, then the author should be willing to demonstrate those things too.

The article establishes a standard. It doesn’t, by itself, prove that the author meets that standard.

I would want to see the supporting evidence.

### 6. What I would want to see from the author

If I were evaluating the marketing expertise being promoted here, I would ask five questions:

| Question | What it reveals |
| --- | --- |
| How do you identify the actual source of a hotel’s OTA dependency? | Diagnostic ability |
| How do you generate incremental direct bookings? | Commercial understanding |
| How do you measure whether your marketing produces incremental revenue? | Measurement competence |
| How do you improve a hotel’s likelihood of appearing in AI recommendations? | Technical understanding |
| Can you demonstrate a before-and-after result and explain what caused the improvement? | Practical execution |

The last question is especially important.

I wouldn’t be particularly impressed by a consultant telling me that a hotel increased its direct bookings by 30%.

I’d want to know:

- What was the original baseline?
- Over what period?
- What changed?
- What else happened during that period?
- How much of the improvement was genuinely incremental?
- How much additional revenue did the hotel retain?
- Could the same methodology work at another property?

That’s the difference between a case study and evidence of a repeatable methodology.

### My final assessment

Intellectual argument

Excellent

The distinction between knowing terminology and understanding mechanisms is powerful and relevant.

Relevance to hospitality

Very high

The problems of OTA dependence, direct demand generation, and AI-driven discovery are real strategic concerns for hotels.

Strength of supporting evidence

Limited in this article

The article makes a persuasive argument but doesn’t provide concrete examples demonstrating the author’s own methodology.

Marketing effectiveness

Strong

It differentiates the author from conventional marketing consultants and creates a memorable positioning statement.

#### The one thing I would add

I think the article is actually pointing toward a much bigger change in the marketing profession.

Historically, information and expertise were valuable partly because they were difficult to access. A consultant who understood marketing concepts, knew the terminology, and could communicate sophisticated strategies had a competitive advantage.

AI is rapidly eroding that advantage.

The next generation of valuable marketing professionals will be those who can combine knowledge with execution, proprietary insight, experimentation, and measurable business outcomes.

In other words:

The value of a marketing expert is no longer what they know or what they can tell you. It’s what they can demonstrably make happen.

And I would add one more sentence:

The ultimate measure of marketing expertise isn’t whether someone can explain why something should work. It’s whether they can make it work, prove that it worked, and explain why.

That’s the standard I would use when evaluating anyone claiming to be an expert in hotel marketing or AI visibility.

And it’s the standard I think the author should be willing to meet as well.
