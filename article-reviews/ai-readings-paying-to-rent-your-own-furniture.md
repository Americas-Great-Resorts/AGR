## The Review

“Paying to Rent Your Own Furniture” asks why a hotel keeps paying a commission when a guest it already knows returns through Expedia. We gave the article’s URL to Google AI, ChatGPT, and Gemini. All three identified the distinction between acquiring a guest and processing another booking. Google AI raised a loyalty objection. ChatGPT questioned the economics and cited research. Gemini expanded the argument with mechanisms and restrictions the article never discusses.

Tell me what your thoughts are on this article: <https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/paying-to-rent-your-own-furniture/>

**AGR’s reading of the responses**

The article distinguishes the commission on a new guest from the commission on someone who already knows the property. It explicitly recognizes Expedia’s reach and calls new-guest acquisition defensible. Its concern is the recurring expense when the hotel has the relationship but fails to reach the returning guest first. It also argues that each booking gives Expedia behavioral data and commission income that strengthen its position for the next booking. The article frames the arrangement as an industry choice. It urges hotels to reach returning guests first without supplying a detailed operating plan.

**Google AI.** Google AI followed the landlord metaphor, the acquisition-versus-transaction distinction, and the account of commissions funding further interception. It framed the article through “Owned Demand,” a term absent from the article’s body, and added a prescription involving first-party data, direct booking incentives, and email. It also supplied a 15–25% commission range in its account of the author’s argument. The article gives no percentage. The phrase it quoted about renting furniture is accurate.

Its loyalty objection identifies something the article leaves unanswered. A guest may choose an OTA for its rewards even after hearing from the hotel. Expedia’s [One Key programme](https://partner.expediagroup.com/en-us/solutions/distribute-your-inventory/one-key-travel-rewards) confirms that the platform offers rewards across eligible travel purchases. Google AI’s booking-experience point identifies another reason a known guest might choose an OTA. A poor direct booking experience would also support the article’s criticism of hotel decisions. The billboard effect concerns OTA listings generating bookings through the hotel’s own channels. It does not explain why a guest keeps booking through an OTA. Google AI’s claim that the reverse happens just as often is unsupported in its response.

**ChatGPT.** ChatGPT recognized that holding guest information and earning direct booking behavior are different things. Its objection lists services Expedia provides. International reach to unfamiliar travelers is a role the article explicitly accepts. Booking convenience, comparison, and payment processing can also serve a repeat guest. The article characterizes that work as processing a transaction without establishing what the service is worth relative to the commission.

The research ChatGPT cited is real. A [2025 Cornell Hospitality Quarterly study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/19389655251318185), using income statements from 644 U.S. hotels, found OTA participation associated with higher profitability for luxury/upscale and midscale properties and lower profitability for economy properties. It uses property-level data from 2019 and does not isolate repeat-guest commissions. Cornell’s [2009 billboard study](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/c9ddec73-3e3a-443b-97be-9467eebb3bcd) found an increase in bookings through the four studied hotels’ own channels when listed on Expedia. Its [2011 follow-up](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/9923ee6a-ac32-48d3-a73e-0fbf26c32137) examined OTA visits before brand-site reservations. Those findings support an economic role for OTA listings. They do not establish whether a particular returning guest’s booking required the platform.

ChatGPT also asked for an implementation strategy. The article supplies the direction of reaching guests first, while leaving the operational detail open. Its observation that AGR sells services relevant to the argument is accurate.

**Gemini.** Gemini called the article commercial satire and rewrote the metaphor as a hotel buying a house and paying to use its couch. The article’s arrangement has the hotel leasing the building while owning the furniture. Gemini’s response also omitted the explicit defense of new-guest commissions and used the terms tax and ransom in its account of OTA costs. That made its reading more broadly hostile to OTAs than the source.

Gemini presented Guest Acquisition Cost, branded search ads, and a Nash equilibrium as parts of the article’s framework. None appears in the article. It also supplied a 15% to 25% commission range and named Owned Demand Infrastructure. Neither appears in the source. Its dependence argument raises a practical question about how freely a hotel can change its distribution. The response supplies no evidence establishing the claimed equilibrium or its assertion that no single hotel can leave. Increasing repeat direct bookings would also allow a hotel to retain OTA distribution for new guests.

Its rate parity claim goes beyond the article and is too broad. Gemini says the clauses strip hotels of the ability to incentivize direct bookings. The [European Commission’s current guidance](https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/factsheet-how-dma-ensures-businesses-using-bookingcom-are-free-set-their-prices-and-bookingcom-2026-09-28_en?prefLang=bg) confirms that accommodation providers in the European Economic Area can offer different prices and conditions through their own channels than on Booking.com. A blanket restriction across hotels and platforms does not hold.

## Google AI Response

The article **“Paying to Rent Your Own Furniture”** presents a compelling, highly critical critique of the luxury hotel industry’s over-reliance on Online Travel Agencies (OTAs) like Expedia. [1]

By using the clever analogy of a **landlord-tenant relationship**, the author strips away the corporate jargon of “distribution strategies” to reveal a harsh financial reality: hotels are repeatedly paying high commissions to re-acquire customers they already own. [1]

Here is a breakdown of the key takeaways, the strengths of the argument, and the practical challenges it introduces:

**Core Takeaways**

- **The “Landlord” Metaphor:** Expedia isn’t trying to destroy hotels; it is simply acting as a landlord collecting rent. The hotel industry willingly handed over the “keys” (guest data, relationships, and booking paths) decades ago for short-term convenience. [1], [2]
- **Acquisition vs. Transaction:** The article correctly differentiates between a *new* customer discovery (where an OTA commission is a justified customer acquisition cost) and a *repeat* guest. Paying a 15–25% commission on a guest who has stayed at a property multiple times is, as the author states, “paying rent on your own furniture.” [1], [2]
- **The Data Flywheel:** Every time a hotel allows a repeat guest to book through an OTA, the OTA captures more behavioral data. They use this data—and the hotel’s own commission money—to get better at intercepting that guest the next time around. [1], [2]

**Why the Argument is Strong**

The article hits the nail on the head regarding **“Owned Demand.”** In digital marketing, there is a massive difference between rented audiences (Google Ads, OTAs, Social Media) and owned audiences (first-party email lists, direct loyalty members). When luxury hotels fail to proactively engage their existing database, they leave a vacuum that OTAs gladly fill. The author rightly points out that this isn’t Expedia being dishonest; it’s a strategic failure on the part of hoteliers. [1]

**The Reality Check (The Counter-Argument)**

While the article’s logic is sound, breaking free from the “landlord” is incredibly difficult in practice for a few reasons:

1. **The Loyalty Loophole:** OTAs have masterfully built their own loyalty rewards ecosystems (like Expedia’s OneKey). A guest might choose to book a luxury hotel through Expedia not because they forgot the hotel exists, but because they want to collect points that they can use later on a flight, a car rental, or a different hotel chain.
2. **Frictionless UX:** OTAs spend billions perfecting the booking user experience. If a hotel’s direct website is clunky, slow, or requires too many steps, a guest will naturally retreat to the ease of an OTA, even if they love the hotel itself.
3. **The Billboard Effect:** Many travelers use Expedia purely as a search engine to discover what exists in an area, then book direct—but the reverse happens just as often.

**The Bottom Line**

The article serves as a blunt wake-up call. For luxury properties, a repeat guest booking through an OTA represents a breakdown in communication and customer relationship management (CRM). To stop “renting their own furniture,” hotels must invest heavily in **first-party data, direct booking incentives, and aggressive email marketing** to reach the guest before the landlord does. [1]

Are you looking at this article from the perspective of a **hotelier/marketer** trying to increase direct bookings, or are you just interested in the **economics of digital platforms**? Let me know so we can discuss specific strategies or dive deeper into the business model!

## ChatGPT Response

My take: I think this article makes a very strong argument, and it identifies a genuine strategic problem in the hotel industry. However, I think it oversimplifies the economics of online travel agencies (OTAs), particularly Expedia.

The central argument is excellent: hotels are sometimes paying commissions to third-party booking platforms for customers they have already acquired, developed relationships with, and earned through their own hospitality.

That’s a real business problem, not just a marketing problem.

Here’s how I would break it down.

### 1. The strongest point: Paying commission for customers you already own

This is the most compelling part of the article.

Consider this scenario:

- A guest discovers a luxury resort through Expedia.
- They stay there and have a wonderful experience.
- They return a second time.
- They return a third time.
- They now know the property, love the experience, and consider it their preferred destination.
- Yet they continue booking through Expedia.

The hotel continues paying a commission on every reservation.

The question the article raises is absolutely legitimate:

Why should a hotel pay an acquisition fee for a customer it has already acquired?

The hotel has invested in the guest experience, built the relationship, maintained the property, and delivered the value that created loyalty.

Expedia may be facilitating the transaction, but it arguably isn’t acquiring that customer again.

This is where hotels can lose substantial long-term profitability.

### 2. The article correctly identifies a problem with customer ownership

One of the most important distinctions in hotel marketing is the difference between:

- Owning the guest relationship.
- Having access to the guest relationship.
- Renting access to the guest through a third-party platform.

These are three very different things.

A hotel might have a sophisticated property management system (PMS), a customer relationship management platform (CRM), email marketing capabilities, and a loyalty program.

But if the guest’s next booking still comes through Expedia, the hotel hasn’t necessarily converted that relationship into direct demand.

The hotel has the customer information, but it hasn’t necessarily earned the customer’s booking behavior.

That’s an important distinction that I think the article gets right.

### 3. Where I disagree: Expedia isn’t simply a landlord

This is where I think the article becomes a little too one-sided.

Expedia provides things that many individual luxury properties cannot easily replicate:

- International exposure and access to travelers who have never heard of the resort.
- Significant search-engine visibility.
- A platform that makes comparing and booking accommodations easy.
- Distribution infrastructure and payment processing.
- Access to customers in markets where the hotel has little existing marketing presence.

In fact, a 2025 peer-reviewed study examining 644 U.S. hotels found that OTA participation was positively associated with occupancy, revenue per available room (RevPAR), and profitability for most hotel categories. The results were not universally positive, but they demonstrate that OTAs can create real economic value.

Sage Journals

There is also something called the billboard effect: travelers discover a hotel on Expedia but subsequently visit the hotel’s website and book directly. Cornell research documented this phenomenon.

Cornell eCommons

So I wouldn’t characterize Expedia as simply collecting rent on something the hotel already owns.

Sometimes Expedia is helping the hotel find customers it otherwise wouldn’t have found.

The real issue is knowing when that service is worth the commission.

### 4. What the article doesn’t sufficiently address: The solution

This is probably my biggest criticism.

The article identifies the problem beautifully, but it doesn’t go far enough in explaining how a hotel actually breaks the cycle.

Simply having a CRM, collecting email addresses, or sending promotional emails isn’t enough.

Hotels need a deliberate strategy to move customers from third-party distribution to direct relationships.

I’d approach this through five initiatives:

1. Capture the relationship. Develop appropriate ways to establish direct communication with guests during their stay, with their permission.
2. Build repeat-booking behavior. Give guests a compelling reason to return through the hotel’s website rather than Expedia.
3. Develop an effective CRM strategy. Use guest preferences, stay history, special occasions, and relevant offers to create meaningful communications.
4. Invest in direct demand generation. Make sure prospective guests discover the property through its own website, content, search presence, social media, and targeted marketing.
5. Measure channel profitability. Track the actual cost of acquiring and retaining guests through each distribution channel, rather than focusing exclusively on occupancy and gross revenue.

The important distinction is that a hotel needs to generate demand, not simply get better at converting demand that originated somewhere else.

### 5. One thing I think is particularly important: The article has a commercial perspective

There’s another dimension worth considering.

The article is published by Americas Great Resorts, a company that offers hotel marketing and demand-generation services. Its website promotes the idea of building owned demand rather than relying on third-party distribution.

That doesn’t make the argument wrong. But it does mean the article is doing two things simultaneously:

- Making a legitimate strategic argument about hotel distribution.
- Establishing the business case for the type of marketing services the company provides.

I would read it as thought leadership with a commercial objective, rather than an entirely neutral analysis.

Americas Great Resorts

That’s an important distinction when evaluating its conclusions.

### 6. My biggest takeaway

I think the most important idea in this article goes beyond Expedia.

It’s about the difference between customer acquisition and customer retention.

A hotel should be willing to pay a commission to acquire a customer it doesn’t have.

But it should be asking much harder questions when it repeatedly pays that same commission to access a customer who already knows the property, has stayed there, and wants to return.

That is where the economics of distribution can become inefficient.

I’d actually take the article’s argument one step further:

The real competitive advantage isn’t having a direct booking engine. It’s creating a situation where customers naturally want to book directly because they’ve developed a relationship with the property.

That’s a much bigger undertaking than simply trying to move bookings away from Expedia.

The article identifies a real problem, uses an effective analogy, and makes a compelling case for hotels to take ownership of their customer relationships. It just needs more economic evidence and a more developed implementation strategy.

### One final thought

I think this article raises a question that every independent luxury resort should be asking:

If Expedia disappeared tomorrow, how many of our existing guests would still know how to find us, remember us, and book with us directly?

If the answer is very few, the hotel has a much bigger problem than OTA commissions.

## Gemini Response

The piece **“Paying to Rent Your Own Furniture”** uses a brilliant piece of commercial satire to highlight a harsh reality in luxury hospitality and hotel distribution: **hotels are effectively paying heavy taxes to intermediaries (Online Travel Agencies, or OTAs) to acquire guests they already own.**

Here is a breakdown of why that analogy hits the nail on the head and what it reveals about the economics of the industry:

### 1. The Core Analogy: Renting What You Built

The metaphor of “paying to rent your own furniture” brilliantly exposes the absurdity of the OTA commission model for repeat guests.

- Imagine buying a house, decorating it with your own furniture, and then paying a middleman a heavy fee every single time you want to sit on your own couch.
- In the hotel world, properties invest millions in capital expenditures, brand identity, service culture, and physical property to build a loyal customer base. Yet, when those loyal, returning guests book through an OTA because of convenience, search dominance, or legacy habits, the hotel hands over 15% to 25% of the revenue. The hotel is essentially paying a landlord fee to access a relationship it paid to create.

### 2. The Illusion of Acquisition

A major theme underlying this framework is that hoteliers often miscalculate **Guest Acquisition Cost (GAC)**. Properties celebrate high volume coming through major third-party platforms without tracking how much of that traffic consists of *their own brand equity* being intercepted. If a guest searches directly for a hotel’s property name, clicks an OTA ad because it out-bids the hotel on Google, and books, the hotel has just paid a steep ransom on a booking that was already intent-driven.

### 3. The Nash Equilibrium Trap

Hotels find themselves trapped in an OTA Nash equilibrium. Individually, no single hotel can afford to pull out of major booking channels because they fear losing occupancy to their neighbors. Collectively, however, they fuel a system that erodes their operating margins and blocks them from owning the guest data. Rate parity clauses further lock hotels into this dynamic, stripping them of the ability to incentivize guests to book direct.

### Summary thoughts

The article cuts straight through the corporate spin of digital distribution. It serves as a sharp wake-up call for luxury hoteliers: **convenience is not an acquisition strategy.** If a hotel doesn’t actively build and protect its **Owned Demand Infrastructure (ODI)**, it will continuously bleed margin, effectively renting its own assets—and its own customers—indefinitely.
