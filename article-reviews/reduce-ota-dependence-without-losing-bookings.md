**AGR’s reading of the responses**

The article applies Nash equilibrium to a hotel that would lose bookings it cannot replace if it delisted while competitors stayed. It separates the equilibrium, which describes the hotel’s incentives, from the controls that operate inside the channel, including rate parity, placement, and auction dynamics. The proposed exit changes the hotel’s options while it remains listed. Content marketing is the visible exterior. Owned Demand Infrastructure (ODI) combines hotel-side identity capture, access to a standing audience of verified affluent travelers, and an introduction before the traveler reaches a booking platform. The article includes AI-mediated discovery among the places where that introduction can happen. The test is whether the hotel can fill the next room it would otherwise depend on the OTA to sell. Average channel share cannot answer that question on its own.

**Gemini.** Gemini recognizes that a hotel can reduce dependence while remaining listed. Its description of capturing and converting guests “through transactional touchpoints” shifts the sequence. If the hotel captures a guest through a stay the OTA delivered, the platform still originated that booking. The article puts the introduction upstream. Gemini also brings in Knowledge Formation Optimization by name. The article discusses how the public record can inform AI discovery. The proposed infrastructure also requires a way for the hotel to capture identity and form a direct relationship.

**Google AI.** Google AI preserves the distinction between content as the exterior and the infrastructure inside it. It also understands how the OTA becomes a supplier the hotel can choose to keep. It goes further when it says hotels can “train modern AI engines” to recommend them. The article describes a public record that can inform how systems understand a property. It gives no evidence that a hotel can train those systems to recommend it. Google AI also says the theory “perfectly explains” 20 years of failed book-direct campaigns. The article offers a structural explanation for why tactics within the channel can sustain the equilibrium. Neither the article nor the response supplies a measurement of those campaigns’ industry-wide results.

Google AI describes AGR as the only entity that can supply the audience. The article makes a narrower claim about what an individual hotel can reproduce in-house and identifies the audience AGR built. Google AI’s point about execution raises a practical question. The article recognizes that identity capture and publishing take time and budget. It leaves open how a hotel would connect its property management system, customer records, and web analytics.

**ChatGPT.** ChatGPT questions whether an established luxury resort and a newly opened independent hotel face the same distribution choices. That difference matters. The article’s marginal-room test gives a way to evaluate it at each property. ChatGPT also challenges the section headed “What the OTA Sees,” which opens with “Nothing.” The article then focuses on whether the OTA’s controls detect where demand originated. ChatGPT points out that an OTA can still observe booking patterns, conversion behavior, and transaction volumes inside its channel. Its observation that the hotel gains leverage by having alternatives aligns with the article’s argument for keeping the option to leave.

The 2025 study ChatGPT cites found positive associations between OTA participation and occupancy, revenue per available room, and EBITDA per available room across 644 U.S. hotels. The 2026 customer lifetime value study found that OTA-acquired guests at a large hotel company generated nearly 20% less lifetime value than direct-acquired guests. Its simulations still found value in investing in both channels. The two studies help explain why a hotel may benefit from keeping the OTA. Neither estimates what a specific hotel could replace with upstream demand.

ChatGPT also accurately cites AGR’s September 2026 study of 148 luxury hotels. Among properties recommended at least once, the study found no detectable association between the measured website AI-readiness variables and recommendation frequency. A model that included Forbes Travel Guide ratings, Michelin Key counts, and market accounted for about 55% of the variation in log recommendation counts. The result describes frequency among hotels already recommended. It does not establish how a hotel enters the shortlist or whether ODI affects recommendations.

ChatGPT’s request for financial proof also holds. Under its illustrative assumptions, shifting $1 million of otherwise identical room revenue from an OTA charging 20% to direct acquisition costing 7% reduces acquisition costs by $130,000 at the same room revenue. That figure depends on those bookings actually displacing OTA bookings. A booking match connects a reservation to a campaign. Establishing whether the reservation added demand, displaced an OTA booking, or would have come direct anyway requires evidence about what would have happened without the campaign.

## Gemini Response

The article **“The Trojan Horse Exit: Escaping the OTA Nash Equilibrium”** (published by *Americas Great Resorts*) takes a sharp, strategic economic view of how luxury hotels, resorts, and hospitality brands interact with Online Travel Agencies (OTAs like Booking.com or Expedia).

Here is a breakdown of the core concepts, strengths, and underlying critiques of the framework presented in the piece:

### 1. Core Premise: The “OTA Nash Equilibrium”

The article frames the relationship between independent luxury properties and OTAs not just as a bad vendor contract, but as a classic game-theoretic **Nash Equilibrium**.

- **The Trap:** Individual hotels know that paying 15% to 25% commissions to OTAs eats into their margins. However, because every competing hotel lists on the OTA to capture immediate search volume and occupancy, no single hotel can unilaterally pull out without losing critical visibility and short-term revenue.
- **The Result:** The market locks into a sub-optimal steady state where everyone hates the high commissions, but nobody can safely defect.

### 2. The “Trojan Horse” Concept

The article’s strategic pivot centers on how to escape this trap without outright “defecting” in a way that starves the property of occupancy. Instead of trying to aggressively cut off OTAs cold turkey—which usually backfires because hotels lack equivalent direct-demand channels—it argues for building **Owned Demand Infrastructure (ODI)** and leveraging alternative visibility layers (such as Knowledge Formation Optimization / AI search positioning).

The “Trojan Horse” metaphor implies that properties must systematically siphon, capture, and convert guest relationships *through* transactional touchpoints until their proprietary demand ecosystem replaces the dependency on the OTA channel entirely.

### Critical Analysis & Thoughts

- **A Clever Application of Game Theory:** Applying John Nash’s non-cooperative equilibrium concept to hotel distribution is conceptually brilliant. It accurately diagnoses *why* smart executives continue to make choices that hurt their long-term bottom line: it’s a systemic coordination problem, not a failure of individual management.
- **Shifting the Burden from “Commission” to “Asset Ownership”:** Many hotel marketing articles whine about the “OTA tax” as a simple cost-reduction problem. This piece correctly frames it as an **asset deficit problem**. OTAs win because they own the customer relationship, data layer, and search intent. Until a hotel builds an equivalent proprietary infrastructure, leaving the OTA is financial suicide.
- **The Nuance of AI and Discovery:** The article ties into broader arguments about how modern discovery layers (like AI-driven travel planning and LLMs) threaten to further entrench aggregator power unless luxury brands aggressively optimize their independent data footprint.

### Summary Verdict

It is an uncommonly sophisticated piece of industry thought leadership. Moving past generic advice like *“spend more money on Google Ads”* or *“build a better website,”* it treats hotel distribution as a structural market design challenge. While executing a “Trojan Horse” exit requires heavy technical and operational discipline (building owned data architectures rather than just renting guests), the diagnostic framework it provides is spot-on.

## Google AI Response

This article presents a sophisticated, game-theoretic perspective on a classic hospitality dilemma: the industry’s costly dependence on Online Travel Agencies (OTAs) like Expedia and Booking.com.

The primary takeaway is that luxury hotels can escape their dependent relationship with OTAs—which functions as a Nash equilibrium where unilateral defection is economic suicide—by building an independent “Owned Demand Infrastructure” disguised as harmless content marketing, allowing them to capture guests upstream before they ever enter the OTA ecosystem. [1]

Here is an objective breakdown of the article’s core arguments, followed by an analysis of its strengths, practical hurdles, and underlying motives.

### 🏛️ The Core Argument: The Trap vs. The Exit

#### 1. The Nash Equilibrium (The Trap)

The author rightly points out that OTA dominance is not due to poor hotel management, but rather a Nash equilibrium resulting from a multi-player Prisoner’s Dilemma. [1], [2]

- The Penalty for Leaving: If a single hotel delists from an OTA, the OTA simply replaces it with a competitor in the search results. The hotel loses massive volume, while the rest of its competitive set remains on the platform. [1]
- Why Optimization Fails: Standard tactics (rate incentives, loyalty perks, metasearch ad spending) fail because they occur inside the OTA’s sandbox. OTAs can easily penalize hotels via placement algorithms or outbid them on keywords using the hotel’s own data. [1], [2]

#### 2. The Trojan Horse (The Exit)

Instead of fighting the OTA within the channel (which triggers algorithmic retaliation), the article suggests changing the hotel’s payoff structure completely by operating upstream of the search engine or OTA interface. [1], [2]

- Camouflage: To the OTA and competitors, a hotel publishing luxury destination guides or thought pieces looks like harmless, low-ROI “content marketing.”
- The Payload: Underneath that content is a system designed to capture guest identities (direct data), leverage pre-existing high-net-worth audiences, and train modern AI engines (like ChatGPT or Google Gemini) to recommend the hotel natively when travelers ask open-ended travel questions. [1]
- The Result: The hotel keeps its OTA listing immaculate and fully compliant, but its baseline reliance on them drops. The OTA is demoted from an inescapable “landlord” to a simple transactional vendor. [1], [2], [3]

### 🧠 Analytical Assessment

#### What the Article Gets Right

- Flawless Application of Game Theory: Framing the OTA problem as a Nash equilibrium is highly accurate. It perfectly explains why twenty years of “book direct” campaigns have failed to move the needle on a macro scale. Individual optimization stabilizes the trap.
- The Reality of Algorithmic Retaliation: The article correctly notes that OTAs aggressively police channel behavior (via rate parity clauses and dimming visibility). Stepping completely outside of their tracking mechanisms is the only way to avoid punishment. [1], [2]
- Forward-Looking on AI Optimization: Pointing out that LLMs/AI search engines draw from a public, structured, and corroborated narrative—and that OTAs have no control over what an AI understands about a property—is an excellent, highly relevant insight for modern digital strategy. [1]

#### The Practical Hurdles & Omissions

- The “Audience” Bottleneck: The author asserts that a hotel cannot build this strategy alone because they lack a 30-year-old proprietary audience database. While building a massive direct-to-consumer audience is incredibly difficult, the article glosses over the massive, sustained capital expenditure required to keep content fresh, engaging, and authoritative enough to stay ahead of AI algorithmic shifts. [1]
- Execution Risk: Transforming content into a high-converting guest identity capture system requires deep technical alignment across a hotel’s tech stack (CRM, Property Management System, and Web Analytics). This is rarely as seamless as the article implies.

### 🎯 The Bottom Line

The article is a brilliantly written piece of thought leadership that doubles as an elegant sales pitch.

The diagnosis of the problem is structurally flawless: hotels cannot win by playing better within the OTA sandbox. However, the final third of the article pivots into a justification for why a hotel must hire Americas Great Resorts (AGR) specifically, claiming that because AGR has owned an exclusive luxury audience since 1993, they are the only entity that can provide the “payload” for the Trojan horse. [1]

Stripping away the marketing pitch, the underlying strategic directive is incredibly sound: Stop fighting the platforms on the distribution layer, and start competing on the narrative and identity layers where platforms are blind. [1]

## ChatGPT Response

My initial reaction: This is a strategically interesting article with a genuinely good central insight, but it also reads like a sophisticated sales argument for the author’s own business model.

I think the author is onto something important about the relationship between luxury hotels and online travel agencies (OTAs), particularly Expedia, Booking.com, and similar platforms. However, I would separate three things:

1. The economic argument about OTA dependency.
2. The proposed strategic solution of building owned demand.
3. The claim that Americas Great Resorts (AGR) has a relatively unique solution to the problem.

Those three things have very different levels of credibility.

Let’s unpack them.

### 1. The Nash equilibrium argument is fundamentally sound

The article’s strongest point is its explanation of why individual hotels struggle to abandon OTAs.

The author describes a situation where every hotel would prefer to pay less commission and own its guest relationships, but no individual hotel can safely abandon the distribution channel while its competitors remain on it.

That’s a legitimate application of game theory.

Consider the economics:

- Hotel A wants to reduce its OTA commissions.
- Hotel B wants the same thing.
- Neither wants to lose bookings while the other continues receiving OTA exposure.
- Both remain on the platforms.
- The platforms retain considerable bargaining power.

This is a classic coordination problem.

The important distinction is that a Nash equilibrium doesn’t necessarily represent the best collective outcome. It simply means that an individual participant has no incentive to change strategy unilaterally, given everyone else’s behavior.

The article explains this particularly well.

I also like the distinction:

> “Everyone knows leaving is suicide. So don’t leave. Stop needing to stay.”

That’s probably the best sentence in the entire article.

It reframes the objective from eliminating OTAs to eliminating dependency on them.

That’s a much more practical business strategy.

### 2. The owned-demand argument is even more important

This is where I think the article gets particularly interesting.

The author argues that hotels shouldn’t necessarily fight the OTA distribution model. Instead, they should build an independent source of demand.

In other words, create a situation where customers discover and select the hotel before they ever visit Expedia or Booking.com.

THE STRATEGIC SHIFT

TRADITIONAL MODEL

Traveler

OTA (Booking.com / Expedia)

Hotel

The hotel pays for access to a customer whose relationship is largely controlled by the intermediary.

OWNED-DEMAND MODEL

Hotel content, audience and reputation

Traveler discovers and chooses hotel

Direct hotel relationship and booking

The hotel influences demand before the customer enters the OTA ecosystem.

This is a meaningful difference.

There is an enormous distinction between competing for customers at the moment they are searching for available rooms and influencing their travel decisions before they begin searching.

The second approach potentially creates much more valuable demand.

And it isn’t limited to hotels. The same principle applies to cruise lines, resorts, vacation ownership, luxury condominiums, and other experience-driven businesses.

### 3. Where I think the article overstates its case

This is where I’d become more cautious.

#### A. The Nash equilibrium analogy is useful, but incomplete

The article treats the hotel industry as though all properties are trapped in essentially the same game.

But hotels are not interchangeable players.

A large luxury resort with a recognizable brand, a loyal customer base, and international awareness has a very different economic position from an independent boutique hotel that opened two years ago.

Their dependence on OTAs is fundamentally different.

Some properties genuinely need OTAs to generate incremental demand. Others are paying commissions on customers who would have booked directly anyway.

That distinction matters.

A 2025 peer-reviewed study examining 644 U.S. hotel properties found that OTA participation was positively associated with occupancy, RevPAR, and EBITDA per available room. That doesn’t mean OTAs are always beneficial, but it demonstrates why simply characterizing them as an economic trap is too simplistic.

Sage Journals

In fact, a 2026 study using hotel customer data found that OTA-acquired customers generated nearly 20% less lifetime value than directly acquired customers, but also concluded that hotels could maximize value by using both channels rather than abandoning one.

Cornell eCommons

My interpretation: The goal shouldn’t be to eliminate OTA dependency at all costs. It should be to optimize the profitability and strategic value of every distribution channel.

#### B. The Trojan Horse is really a customer acquisition strategy

The article’s most important commercial proposition is that content is the vehicle for building owned demand.

But content alone doesn’t create demand.

The author acknowledges this, which is why AGR’s proposition includes three components:

- Guest identity capture.
- Access to an established audience of affluent travelers.
- Upstream introduction to travelers before they reach a booking platform.

The third component is particularly interesting.

However, I would want to understand the second component much more carefully.

The article claims AGR has maintained an affluent traveler audience since 1993 that individual hotels cannot replicate.

That’s potentially a significant competitive advantage.

But there are questions I would want answered:

- How large is that audience?
- How many are active, verified travelers?
- What percentage are actually qualified prospects for a particular property?
- How frequently are they exposed to a hotel’s content?
- What percentage subsequently book?
- How much of that demand is genuinely incremental?

An audience accumulated over 30 years is not automatically valuable. Its value depends on engagement, purchasing intent, relevance, and measurable commercial outcomes.

The real asset isn’t the size of the audience. It’s the ability to convert that audience into profitable, incremental bookings.

I’d want to see that demonstrated.

#### C. The claim that OTAs cannot see or respond to this strategy is overstated

The author argues that because hotels remain listed, maintain rate compliance, and don’t change their channel behavior, the OTA has no reason to retaliate.

There is some logic here.

But I wouldn’t take it as an absolute.

OTAs may not directly observe where a traveler’s initial interest originated. However, they can observe booking patterns, conversion behavior, customer behavior, and changes in transaction volume.

More importantly, if a hotel develops a substantial independent customer acquisition capability, the OTA doesn’t necessarily need to retaliate.

It can simply become less important.

That’s actually the more compelling argument.

The hotel wins because it has alternatives, not because it has discovered a way to become invisible to the OTA.

### 4. The AI component could be the most important part of the entire thesis

This is where I think the article is looking ahead.

Travel discovery is changing.

Historically, a traveler might:

1. Search Google for luxury resorts in Hawaii.
2. Visit TripAdvisor.
3. Open Booking.com or Expedia.
4. Compare properties.
5. Make a reservation.

Increasingly, travelers can ask AI systems questions such as:

- What is the best luxury resort in Maui for a couple?
- Where should I stay in the Caribbean for a private beachfront experience?
- Which luxury hotels offer exceptional wellness programs?
- What is the best resort for a multigenerational family vacation?

The AI system can influence the shortlist before the traveler ever reaches a booking website.

The Westin Maui Resort & Spa, Ka’anapali | Costco Travel

Exclusive Offerat Le Barthélemy Hotel & Spa |Best Caribbean Hotel

Top Trends in Luxury Resorts That Will Redefine Travel by…

This creates an interesting new competitive battleground.

A hotel that is accurately understood, described, and represented across credible public sources may have an advantage in AI-mediated discovery.

But there’s an important qualification: nobody outside the AI providers can guarantee exactly how a particular hotel will be selected or recommended.

AGR itself acknowledges some of these limitations in its published research. Its September 2026 study of 148 luxury hotels found no detectable relationship between several measured website AI-readiness variables and recommendation frequency among hotels already recommended at least once.

Americas Great Resorts

That’s an important distinction.

Being technically optimized for AI doesn’t necessarily mean AI will recommend you.

The real opportunity is broader: building a strong, consistent, credible digital presence that makes the property easier to understand and recommend.

I think this is a legitimate strategic opportunity, but it is still an evolving discipline rather than a guaranteed distribution channel.

### 5. The biggest thing missing: actual financial proof

If I were evaluating this article as a hotel owner, investor, or hospitality executive, I’d want to move beyond the theory.

Let’s imagine a luxury resort with $10 million in annual room revenue.

Suppose:

- 40% of bookings originate through OTAs.
- Average OTA commission is 20%.
- The hotel generates $4 million in OTA bookings.

That’s $800,000 in annual OTA commissions.

Illustrative assumptions, not AGR client results.

Now suppose the hotel develops an independent demand-generation program that produces $1 million in genuinely incremental direct bookings.

At an assumed 7% acquisition cost, the cost would be $70,000.

The hotel would retain approximately $930,000 before room operating costs and other associated expenses.

That’s attractive.

But there’s a critical distinction:

If those $1 million in direct bookings simply replace bookings that would otherwise have arrived through the OTA, the savings are different.

The real financial analysis needs to account for:

- Incremental room revenue.
- Displaced OTA commissions.
- Direct marketing costs.
- Audience acquisition costs.
- Technology and CRM costs.
- Booking conversion rates.
- Repeat guest value.

This is where I would insist on independently verifiable attribution.

The article mentions deterministic attribution, which is encouraging, but I’d want to see actual client-level results rather than just the description of the methodology.

### 6. My biggest reservation about AGR’s business proposition

There’s something worth recognizing about the article’s construction.

It first establishes that the hotel industry has a structural problem.

Then it argues that traditional solutions don’t work.

Then it introduces a solution requiring three specific capabilities.

Finally, it explains why AGR has the audience and infrastructure that hotels cannot build themselves.

That’s very effective positioning.

It also means the article is not an independent academic examination of the OTA industry. It is a strategic argument published by a company that sells the proposed solution.

That doesn’t make the argument wrong.

But it means I’d want independent validation of the commercial claims.

In particular, I’d want to see:

- Actual hotel case studies with before-and-after results.
- Verified direct-booking revenue attributable to AGR.
- Customer acquisition costs compared with OTA commissions.
- Incremental bookings versus bookings that would have occurred anyway.
- Retention and repeat-booking performance.
- Results across multiple properties, not just selected success stories.

The distinction between an intellectually compelling strategy and a commercially proven strategy is enormous.

Here’s how I’d evaluate the article’s major propositions:

### 8. What I would take away from this article

I actually think the most important idea is somewhat bigger than the article itself.

The future competitive advantage in hospitality may belong to the companies that control the relationship with the traveler before the traveler decides where to book.

Think about what that means.

For decades, hotels have competed over:

- Location.
- Amenities.
- Room quality.
- Pricing.
- Brand recognition.
- Distribution.

But there’s another competitive asset emerging: control over customer discovery and consideration.

Who introduces the traveler to the property?

Who influences the traveler’s perception?

Who owns the relationship?

Who gets to influence the final decision?

And who ultimately gets paid when that traveler books?

These questions become increasingly important as AI changes how consumers discover products, services, and destinations.

I would argue that the underlying strategy isn’t really about beating Expedia or Booking.com.

It’s about reducing the number of intermediaries between a business and its customer.

That’s a much bigger opportunity.

### One final thought

If I were sitting across the table from the author of this article, I’d ask one question:

“Can you show me three luxury hotels where your owned-demand infrastructure has measurably reduced OTA dependency, increased net room revenue, and produced a higher lifetime value per guest—and can you prove that the results were incremental?”

If the answer is supported by convincing financial data, I’d take this business model very seriously.

If the answer relies primarily on audience size, publishing activity, website traffic, or AI visibility reports, I’d be considerably more cautious.

The distinction is between selling marketing infrastructure and actually delivering economic value.

My verdict: The article contains a very good strategic thesis. I agree with its central premise that hotels should build demand they control rather than simply optimize their dependence on intermediaries. I am less convinced that AGR has demonstrated that its particular solution is the answer.

And I would not dismiss the article simply because it is promotional. There’s a genuinely important idea here that hospitality executives should be thinking about.

One source worth examining alongside the article is the [2025 peer-reviewed study of OTA participation and hotel profitability](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/19389655251318185). It provides an important counterbalance to the argument that OTA dependency is necessarily bad.

I would be particularly interested in exploring whether this strategy could fundamentally change the economics of luxury hospitality over the next five years, especially as AI becomes a more significant travel-discovery channel.
