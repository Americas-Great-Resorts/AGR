**The OTA gets credit for the booking. The hotel gets the commission invoice. The AI recommendation that put the hotel on the shortlist never comes up at the commercial review.**

Nobody funds a problem that doesn't show up on the P&L. AI visibility has no line item, so it keeps getting pushed to next quarter. The commercial team can't see it, so it ignores it.

The problem is still there. It just books under somebody else's name.

That's zero-click AI search from the hotel's side of the desk. The traveler gets her answer from ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity, Copilot, Google AI or any other AI assistant. She may click out of the answer to Google, Tripadvisor or an OTA. She may not click at all. Either way, the hotel never sees an AI referral. The demand is real. The hotel just can't see where it came from.

## Three Ways AI Demand Disappears

A traveler planning a Miami vacation asks an AI assistant for a beachfront hotel with a good pool and restaurants within walking distance. The assistant names three properties. From there the demand goes one of three ways, and none of them leave an AI fingerprint on the hotel's books.

**One. The hotel is recommended, and an intermediary supplies the next page.** The answer's link often doesn't go to the hotel. It goes to a Google hotel page, a Tripadvisor listing that hands the booking to an OTA, a travel publisher or an OTA. She reads reviews, compares rates and books through an online travel agency. The hotel records the revenue and pays the commission. Its analytics show no AI visit. At the commercial review, the OTA delivered the guest. The report knows who took the reservation. It has no idea who made the introduction.

**Two. The hotel is recommended, and the guest finds her own way back.** She remembers the name and searches for it on Google. That shows up as organic search. She calls the reservations desk. That shows up as a phone booking. She hands the name to her travel advisor or her Amex travel service. That shows up as an advisor booking. Every time, the credit goes to the last stop in the chain. The conversation where she first heard the hotel's name doesn't appear in any report.

**Three. The hotel isn't recommended at all.** This is the expensive one. The traveler never hears the name. She books one of the three properties the AI did name. The hotel gets no booking, no lead, no abandoned cart, no visit. A competitor gets the guest, and nothing in the hotel's data records it. No report carries a line for the guest who never knew you existed. For what that looks like from the inside, read [The Hotel That Wasn't There](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/hotel-that-wasnt-there-ai-agents/).

The first two cases misattribute demand. The third erases it.

## "But AI Traffic Is Tiny"

Somebody at the commercial review will say it. They'll be right about the traffic and wrong about the conclusion.

On its November 3, 2026 earnings call, Booking Holdings management said external AI referrals were **significantly less than 1% of Booking.com room nights.** Adobe found AI traffic to U.S. travel websites up **119%** year over year in July 2026. Both figures are reported in [AGR's AI hotel link study](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-link-study/).

Referral counts show which visits arrived from AI. They miss the traveler who reads the answer and finds the hotel another way. A traveler who meets a hotel in ChatGPT and books it three days later through Google, the phone or an OTA registers as a Google visitor, a phone booking or an OTA reservation. She doesn't register as an AI referral. A low AI referral count doesn't show that AI isn't doing much. It shows that nobody is counting what AI does.

## Where AI-Discovered Travelers Go Next

[TubStays surveyed 5,000 U.S. travelers](https://www.tubstays.com/research/ai-agents-travel-survey-questionnaire-and-results.pdf) in a weighted sample of adults who took an overnight trip in the past year. It asked the 2,520 who had used AI for a trip task how far the AI went the last time:

- **61%** took its ideas or information and chose and booked everything themselves
- **12%** booked something it recommended by going to the website or app themselves
- **6%** booked something it recommended through a link it gave them

Twice as many travelers went around the AI's link as went through it. That's zero-click, measured.

TubStays also asked what travelers did the last time an AI recommended a place to stay. Of 1,517 who'd had a recommendation:

- **51%** searched on Google or another search engine
- **49%** read guest reviews
- **45%** visited the property's own website
- **43%** looked it up on a booking site
- **41%** looked at photos
- **36%** clicked the links or sources the AI gave

Clicking the AI's own links ranked behind five other checks. Most travelers left the conversation and went somewhere else to verify. Each of those somewhere-elses takes the credit.

Of the 705 travelers who booked the most recent stay AI helped them choose, **39% booked through an online travel agency and 30% booked on the property's own site or app.** AI helped them choose the stay. The OTA or the property took the booking. Neither one's report says AI.

## How Many Guests Meet the Hotel in AI?

The question that matters to a hotel isn't where the trip started. It's where the guest first heard the hotel's name. Those are different events.

TubStays found that **8% of the 3,426 travelers asked said an AI assistant or agent was where they first looked for a place to stay** on their most recent trip. In [Phocuswright's *The AI Surge* report](https://www.phocuswire.com/ai-surge-us-behavioral-shift-travel-phocuswright-research-2026), **generative AI platforms such as ChatGPT reached 33% usage for trip research.** Both measure the trip.

[AGR's November 2026 survey](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/luxury-traveler-ai-survey/) of its high-net-worth luxury traveler audience asked about the hotel: "In the past 12 months, did you book a hotel you first heard about from an AI assistant?" Of the 2,768 who answered, **1,530, or 55.3%, said yes.**

The measures don't contradict each other. A traveler can start her trip on Google and meet a particular hotel for the first time in ChatGPT halfway through. The hotel's referral report records neither the start nor the introduction.

Booking inside the AI is still rare. In the same AGR survey, 50 of the 2,381 respondents who answered the booking-channel question, **2.1%**, booked their most recent luxury stay inside an AI assistant. A hotel waiting for AI to show up as a booking channel will wait a long time.

## The Answer Often Sends the Traveler Somewhere Else

[AGR's link study](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-link-study/) tested ChatGPT, Gemini and Google AI Mode. **536 of the 899 answers that named at least one hotel had no link to an official hotel or hotel-brand website, in either the answer text or the citations.**

Each engine has its own favorite intermediary:

- **Google AI Mode** sent 95.7% of its citation links to Google's own pages. It did put a hotel website link in the answer text of 166 of 300 answers.
- **ChatGPT** sent 47.2% of its citation links to publishers and 21.0% to Tripadvisor. Tripadvisor was the recorded provider for **95.0% of ChatGPT's hotel map pins.**
- **Gemini** gave no citations at all in 34.3% of its answers. When it did cite, 62.4% of the links went to publishers.

OTAs got 3.1% of ChatGPT's citation links and 0.3% of Google AI Mode's. The intermediaries that dominate are Google, Tripadvisor and publishers. That doesn't let the OTA off the hook. It moves the OTA one step down the chain. Tripadvisor is a [metasearch site](https://www.cloudbeds.com/articles/level-the-playing-field-with-metasearch-advertising/) that hands the booking to an OTA or the hotel. The hotel earned the recommendation, and somebody else wrote the next page the traveler read.

Travelers still want the source. In [AGR's survey](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/luxury-traveler-ai-survey/), **59.8% of all submissions selected the hotel's own website** as a place they check when AI recommends a hotel. No option was selected more often. The traveler is looking for the front door. Whether she finds it is up to the hotel.

## A Zero-Click Attribution Model for Hotels

The hotel's systems won't tell it where demand started. The guest will, if somebody asks.

Add two questions to the post-stay survey for first-time guests:

1. **Where did you first hear about this hotel?**
2. **Where did you go next to learn more or check rates?**

List AI assistants as an answer, and let guests name the one they used: ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity, Copilot, Google AI or another AI assistant. Have the reservations team ask the first question on the phone. Match every answer to the reservation.

| What to record | What it tells the hotel | Miami example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guest-reported discovery source | Where the guest first heard about the property | AI assistant |
| Guest-reported next step | Where the guest went to evaluate it | Tripadvisor listing |
| Recorded booking channel | Who took the reservation | Online travel agency |
| Recorded distribution cost | What the hotel paid for the booking | OTA commission |

It's recall data from guests who stayed. It's imperfect. It's still more than the hotel knows today.

At the commercial review, report how many first-time guests answered, how many named AI, how those guests booked, the room revenue attached to them and the commissions paid on them. Discovery and distribution cost end up on the same page.

The survey can't reach Scenario Three. Guests who never heard of the hotel don't fill out its post-stay survey. That takes a second measurement, reported at the same meeting: ask the AI systems the questions travelers ask before they know the hotel's name, run them repeatedly, and record how often the hotel appears and who appears instead.

**The survey tells you how booked guests found you. The answer tests show where your hotel was missing.**

## Where KFO Fits

Finding the gap isn't fixing it. Some of what the tests turn up is plain data accuracy. The answer has the wrong pool hours or a restaurant that closed years ago. Fix the listings and the official site.

The bigger problems are a different class. The hotel is persistently absent from answers it should be in. It shows up subordinated behind competitors, or described as something it isn't. That's where [**Knowledge Formation Optimization (KFO)**](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/kfo-knowledge-formation-optimization/) applies. KFO is AGR's discipline for structuring, sequencing, distributing, corroborating and correcting the public source record AI systems draw on, then measuring whether those systems reproduce it accurately across repeated queries over time.

The sequence is simple. Run the unbranded questions. Save the dated answers. Separate the data errors from the absences. Correct what the hotel controls. Rerun the same questions and see what moved.

## The Introduction Layer

AI has become an introduction layer that sits outside the attribution systems hotels rely on. The hotel that measures it gets to see it. The hotel that waits for it to show up on the P&L will keep paying commission on guests it never knew AI sent.

## What Waiting Until Next Quarter Costs

By the time AI makes the agenda at the next quarterly meeting, a lot of travelers will already have asked where to stay in Miami. The hotel can fix its information later. It can't go back and get onto a shortlist a traveler already booked from.

A single check won't settle anything either. In [AGR's link study](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-link-study/), **at least one hotel was added or removed across three same-day runs in 292 of 299 complete question-and-engine groups.** The answers changed within a single 91-minute collection window. A hotel that checked once in the spring knows what one answer looked like in the spring.

Every hotel is in the AI answers or out of them right now, whether anyone checks or not. Putting it off to the next quarterly meeting doesn't pause anything. It's putting on the blindfold, plugging your ears and duct-taping your mouth while the hotel down the street audits its site, fixes its listings, tests the answers and fixes them again. By the time the blindfold comes off, they're on their third round. You're on your first. And the guests who booked in between aren't coming back.

The report tests local recommendation answers in **ChatGPT, Gemini and Google AI Mode**. It shows whether the hotel appeared, which competitors were named, how accurately the property was described and where property-related links led. It also checks sampled pages of the official site and suggests fixes. Download the PDF as a dated first reading to test against next time.
