## The Article

<!-- agr:published-article-start -->

“Nobody goes there anymore. It’s too crowded.”

The line is attributed to Yogi Berra. People laugh because it makes no sense. It makes perfect sense. It’s the most accurate sentence ever spoken about luxury.

Luxury isn’t a thing. It’s a distance. The distance between you and everyone else who wanted it and didn’t get it. Shrink the distance and the thing is still there. The luxury isn’t.

Now give every traveler the same concierge. Let it answer the same question the same way for everyone who asks it. Then act surprised when the velvet rope is lying on the floor.

## The concierge used to whisper

For generations, luxury travel was discovered by whisper.

A friend at dinner. A travel advisor who’d been there. The concierge in Paris who knew a man in Positano. The whisper was the filter. It moved through people who resembled each other. It arrived late, arrived partial and arrived to almost nobody. That was the point. A secret told to the right twelve people stays a secret. It just gets better reservations.

The whisper did something nobody wrote down. It matched guests to places. The friend who told you about the inn in Vermont knew you. She knew you’d love the silence and hate the cell service. The recommendation came with a fit test built in.

AI assistants (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity, Copilot, Google AI Mode, or any other one you like) also whisper. Privately. Conversationally. One to one. It feels like the old concierge.

It isn’t. It’s a **broadcast whisper**. An intimate tip that can be delivered, word for word, to everyone who asks the same question. It has the tone of a secret and the reach of a Super Bowl ad.

One catch, and it’s the whole article. The broadcast depends on the question. Ask the generic question and the machine behaves like a consensus engine. Ask anything else and it behaves like a slot machine. Hold that thought. It’s the way out.

## Luxury is a positional good. Machines can’t count to one.

In 1899 Thorstein Veblen gave us “conspicuous consumption.” The rich buy things partly so other people can see they bought them.

In 1976 the economist Fred Hirsch went further. He described **positional goods**: things whose value depends on other people not having them. The corner office. The empty beach. The table nobody else can get. You can’t mass-produce a positional good. Produce more and you haven’t made more of it. You’ve destroyed it.

Luxury hotels sell positional goods and pretend they sell rooms. The thread count is real. The real product is the absence of the crowd.

Now look at what an AI recommendation is. It can be repeated forever. The assistant can recommend the last available suite to every single person who asks. The hotel can sell it once.

That’s the problem in one sentence. **The recommendation is the most valuable thing a luxury hotel can receive and the easiest thing a machine can give.**

And here’s the distinction the industry keeps skipping. The machine is built to find more guests. Luxury is built to find the right guests. Those are not the same objective. They’re barely the same sport.

## Sometimes the machine has a favorite

AGR asked ChatGPT, Google AI Mode, Gemini and Copilot 100 luxury travel questions across 10 destinations, three times each, on September 27, 2026. We kept 1,177 answers. The [full study is here](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-recommendations-research/).

In two cities, the machine crowned a king. The Peninsula Chicago appeared in 96 of 118 Chicago answers. Halekulani appeared in 91 of 118 Honolulu answers. [Five weeks later](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-recommendations-five-weeks-later/) they were at 32 of 40 and 33 of 40. That’s a coronation.

The other eight cities had leaders, not kings. Mandarin Oriental, New York led with 50 of 120. The Setai led Miami with 51 of 117. Three of the ten leaders changed in five weeks. One led by a single appearance.

And under the leaders, chaos. Of 379 question-and-engine groups asked three times on the same day, only 16 returned the same set of hotels all three times. The other 363 shuffled. An average answer named about five hotels. The rest of the guest list was written in pencil.

So the AI era doesn’t produce one market. It produces three. A few hotels get crowned. A few more lead on points. Everybody else plays a lottery they didn’t enter and can’t see the results of.

Robert Merton borrowed a line from the Gospel of Matthew to describe how credit piles up on the already famous: to those who have, more is given. He called it the Matthew effect. Where the machine crowns a hotel, it looks a lot like Merton’s scientists. Fame begets mentions. Mentions beget fame.

## The coronation curse

Every hotel wants the crown. Nobody has asked what it weighs.

Here’s a bet.

The crowned hotel isn’t recommended to its guest. It’s recommended to everyone who asked the default question. “Best luxury hotel in Chicago” is the most generic sentence in travel. The person typing it could be your future twenty-year regular. Could be someone who wants a scene and has no idea you’re the quiet one.

Fame isn’t the danger. Plenty of famous hotels stay exclusive. A hotel controls its own inventory. The danger is the recommendation that leaves out the reason to go.

Suppose the machine keeps recommending a quiet hotel to travelers who want a scene. The hotel fills. The guests who wanted a scene wonder where everybody is. The regulars wonder what happened to the bar. Every room sold. Everybody disappointed.

That’s the **coronation curse**. A hotel can sell every room and still sell them to the wrong people. The risk is that the machine doesn’t send you more of your guest. It sends you more of its guest.

It’s a bet, so here’s how to settle it. A crowned hotel should watch three numbers after the crown arrives: the share of first-time guests, the share of repeat guests, and the gap between what guests expected and what they wrote in the review. If first-timers climb, repeats slide and the reviews start complaining about the very thing the hotel was built to be, the curse is real. If not, enjoy the crown.

## Maya Bay had a government

We’ve seen what mass discovery does to a place with an open door.

Maya Bay in Thailand was a quiet cove until the 2000 film *The Beach* put it on every wish list. By the late 2010s, [as many as 4,000 people arrived daily on boats](https://time.com/6204958/thailand-travel-phi-phi-maya-bay/). Authorities closed it in June 2018 to save what was left of the reef. It was supposed to be a few months. [It reopened in January 2022](https://www.lonelyplanet.com/articles/maya-bay-the-beach-thailand-closed), with visitors limited to 380 an hour.

The beach famous for being undiscovered had to be closed by the state because everyone discovered it. Then it got a literal velvet rope.

Maya Bay had a government to install the rope. Your hotel has price. Price is a terrible rope. It selects for money and nothing else. Luxury was never only about money. Ask anyone who’s watched a lobby fill up with people who can afford it and don’t get it.

## Outsourced taste is a receipt

In 1979 Pierre Bourdieu argued that taste is a class marker. Your taste places you. Knowing the right place before everyone else is how the people who know signal that they know.

Now outsource taste to a machine that gives everyone who asks the same question the same answer.

Taste stops distinguishing anyone. If the machine picked it, you didn’t. Outsourced taste isn’t taste. It’s a receipt.

When taste becomes a commodity, discernment becomes the luxury. And affluent travelers are acting like they know it.

AGR surveyed its high-net-worth luxury traveler audience in November 2026 and received 3,562 submissions. The [full survey is here](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/luxury-traveler-ai-survey/). Of the 2,768 who answered the question, 55.3% said they’d booked a hotel they first heard about from an AI assistant in the past year. So they use it. A lot.

Then look at what they do next. Across all 3,562 submissions, 59.8% picked the hotel’s own website as a place they check when AI recommends a hotel. Asked to name up to two sources they trust most for the final decision, only 8.0% picked an AI assistant.

They let the machine nominate. They don’t let it elect.

Discernment didn’t die. It moved one step downstream, to the hotel’s own website, where the guest checks the machine’s work. That’s where you actually make the sale.

## The describability trap

In 2016 the critic Kyle Chayka named something people had noticed and never labeled. [He called it “AirSpace”](https://www.theverge.com/2016/8/3/12325104/airbnb-aesthetic-global-minimalism-startup-gentrification): the same reclaimed wood, Edison bulbs and faux-artisanal minimalism in coffee shops from Brooklyn to Seoul. Nobody coordinated it. Platforms rewarded it, so everyone converged on it.

Here’s the AI-era version.

Machines recommend what they can describe. A hotel that fits in one clean sentence (“oceanfront, adults-only, Michelin-starred, walking distance to the old town”) is easy to recommend. A hotel whose magic is the way the light hits the hallway at four in the afternoon, or the bartender who remembers your father, is hard to describe. Hard to describe is hard to recommend.

So expect pressure. Hotels that want to be recommended will drift toward what’s easy to summarize. Clear categories. Standard amenities. Signature this, curated that. Every property reshaped into a tidy sentence.

That’s the **describability trap**. Legibility selects for blandness. Do it industry-wide and you get AirSpace with a turndown service.

Here’s the way out, and it matters for everything that follows. The trap isn’t being described. The trap is changing the hotel to fit a familiar label. Keep the bartender. Keep the strange beautiful thing. Then describe it so precisely that the right person recognizes it instantly. “Wellness retreat” describes ten thousand places. “No music at the pool, ever” describes yours. Don’t make the hotel simpler. Make the description sharper.

## The most exclusive hotel in Miami doesn’t exist

One more data point, because the universe has a sense of humor.

In AGR’s [Miami language study](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-recommendations-by-language/) on November 17, 2026, Mandarin Oriental, Miami was recommended in 23 answers. It closed May 31, 2025. It’s been demolished.

Zero rooms. Zero guests. Twenty-three recommendations. The only hotel in Miami with a truly impenetrable velvet rope is one nobody can get into, and the machine keeps sending people to the door.

## The rope moved. It’s standing at the question now.

Here’s what the industry is missing.

The velvet rope never disappeared. It moved. It used to stand at the door. Then it stood at the price. Now it stands at the **question**.

AGR’s own data shows it. In Orlando, Four Seasons Orlando led overall with 77 of 120 answers. Ask for luxury discovery, though, and Ritz-Carlton Orlando, Grande Lakes appeared in all 12 answers. Ask for boutique hotels and The Delaney Hotel appeared in all 12. Three different questions. Three different kings. Same city, same day.

The machine gives the generic question a generic answer. Change the question and the answer changes. Ask a specific, idiosyncratic question, the kind only your actual guest would ask, and you’re in a different room entirely.

“Quiet hotel in Chicago where a writer could work for a week without being recognized.” “Hawaii resort where my mother, who uses a cane, can reach the beach without stairs.” “Somewhere in the Keys with serious fly fishing and no children’s program.”

Those questions are the new velvet rope. They screen the asker before the machine says a word. The person who asks them is already your guest. They just haven’t found you yet.

So the luxury strategy for the AI era isn’t “be the answer.” It’s **be the answer to the question only your guest would ask.** Lose the generic question with dignity. Win the specific one completely.

## Tell the machine who you’re not for

Every hotel tells the machine who it’s for. Couples. Families. Wellness. Adventure. Everybody. Hotels describe themselves the way a politician answers a question: broadly enough to offend no one and specifically enough to mean nothing.

Luxury has always been defined as much by what it refuses as by what it offers. No children under 16. No buyouts. No DJ at the pool. No phones in the dining room. No.

Every “no” is a stanchion. Put them in the public record, stated plainly and repeated consistently, and the machine has what it needs to do your door work. The bachelorette party gets routed to the place with the DJ. The family of seven gets routed to the kids’ club. The guest who asked for silence gets routed to you.

Call it **negative positioning for machines**. Teach the AI who you’re not for, so it stops sending them.

This is what Knowledge Formation Optimization was built for. [KFO](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/knowledge-formation-optimization-kfo/) means deciding what AI systems should know about you before anyone asks. Most people hear that and think it means getting mentioned more. It means getting described exactly. Including the exclusions. A precise description is a velvet rope made of facts.

## Keep a room the machine can’t see

The machine works from what’s public. That’s your opening.

Luxury always lived partly in what wasn’t public. The rate offered to the right traveler. The suite that never shows on the booking engine. The invitation. The whisper.

So the rope goes in two places.

**The public layer:** precise, consistent, honest about who you’re for and who you aren’t. That’s where KFO works. It shapes the facts the broadcast whisper is built from.

**The private layer:** a direct line to affluent travelers, opted in, outside every platform. Not just your past guests. New ones who have never stayed with you and never typed your name into anything. That’s [Owned Demand Infrastructure](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/how-owned-demand-is-built-luxury-hotels/). On that channel the hotel chooses the audience, the offer and the moment. The machine doesn’t get a vote. It’s [the only room in the building Expedia can’t book](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/luxury-hotel-email-no-auction/). It’s also the one invitation the guy who typed the generic question never receives.

The public layer decides who knocks. The private layer decides who gets invited.

## The map

Put it all together and every luxury property sits on two axes. Which questions name you: the generic ones or the specific ones. And how precisely the machine describes you once it does.

|  | **Described precisely** | **Described vaguely** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Named for the generic question** | **The Crowned.** Famous and described right. A good place to be. The machine granted it, and the machine can take it back. The rope is price. | **The Miscast.** Wins the volume, loses the plot. Sent travelers who wanted something else. This is where the coronation curse lives. |
| **Named for the specific question** | **The Chosen.** The answer to the question your guest actually asks. Found by the right person, verified on your own website, booked there. The rope is intact. | **The Footnote.** Shows up for the right question, described so loosely the right guest can’t tell it’s theirs. Close. Not close enough. |

Here’s why the Chosen beats the Crowned, even though the Crowned gets more mentions.

You can’t build a coronation. The machine hands it out, on its own schedule, for its own reasons. Three of ten crowns changed hands in five weeks. You can build being chosen. Every specific fact, every stated exclusion, every precise description is a brick you lay yourself. One is a lottery win. The other is a foundation.

That’s not invisibility. It’s precision. It’s the difference between being famous and being known.

## Nobody goes there anymore

For thirty years hotels fought to be found. Search, then social, then OTAs, then AI. Every channel promised more eyes. Every one delivered them.

Luxury was never about being found. It was about being found by the right people. The machine didn’t change that. It just made it the only question left.

Yogi was right. The crowd is the end of the place.

The velvet rope didn’t disappear. It moved.

Make sure the machine is sending your guest.
