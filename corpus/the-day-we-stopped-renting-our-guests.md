## The Article

<!-- agr:published-article-start -->

**Hotel OTA dependence does not always persist because executives misunderstand the economics. Sometimes they understand the commission burden, recognize the need for direct guest acquisition, and still postpone building an alternative source of demand.** The property continues filling rooms. Revenue appears stable. The cost of waiting remains difficult to see until the distribution mix begins moving in the wrong direction.

*Editorial note: This is an illustrative hotel leadership scenario, not a documented AGR client case study. The executives, conversations, timeline, and numerical results are constructed to examine the commercial decisions and measurement problems involved in reducing intermediary dependence. Documented AGR client results are linked separately.*

The presentation lasted forty-five minutes. The math was clear. The mechanism was explained. The CMO understood both.

She went back to her desk and did nothing.

Not because she disagreed. Not because the numbers were wrong. Because the thing being described was going to take time, the current quarter was already complicated, ownership had questions about the renovation budget, and eighteen months felt like a different administration’s problem.

The OTA commissions were manageable. Occupancy was holding. The RevPAR comparison was acceptable.

Nothing was on fire.

---

## The Number That Changed the Conversation

Twelve months later, the revenue manager flagged it in a Tuesday morning meeting.

He had been watching it for two quarters and had finally put it in a slide: OTA-sourced room revenue as a percentage of total room revenue, trending upward.

Not necessarily because the property was performing worse. The direct channel was flat while the OTA channel accounted for a growing portion of the business.

The CMO recognized the slide immediately. Same mechanism, different source. The presentation she had ignored twelve months earlier was now arriving through the revenue manager’s distribution report.

The difference was that this time the number belonged to them. Not an agency’s projection. Not a framework applied to a hypothetical property. Their commission expense, increasing without anyone having made an explicit decision to increase dependence on the channel.

She said she wanted to revisit the acquisition conversation.

The revenue manager said he remembered the presentation. His tone suggested he had not filed it under solutions.

She called a meeting anyway.

The economics are straightforward, even when the decision to act is not. OTA commissions are measurable, but the financial benefit of developing an independent source of demand depends on acquisition cost, booking contribution, and the proportion of reservations that would otherwise have come through intermediaries. AGR examines those tradeoffs in [How Hotels Reduce OTA Dependence and Increase Direct Bookings](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/reducing-ota-dependence-in-luxury-hospitality-the-real-economics-of-direct-booking-strategy/).

## The Conversation Nobody Writes About

It took three meetings, not one.

The first was with the CFO. He came prepared with the right question and did not like the answer.

How long before a new acquisition program could be evaluated? How much would the first campaign cost? Would any bookings be genuinely additional, or would the campaign merely receive credit for travelers who intended to book anyway?

The CMO had no defensible timetable for when an independent source of demand would become financially meaningful. Initial campaign activity could be measured relatively quickly. Changes in repeatable demand origin, channel mix, and long-term acquisition economics required a different measurement period.

“So we’re spending money before we know whether it will work.”

“We’re spending money to find out whether we can originate more of our own demand. But yes.”

He did not approve it that afternoon.

He asked which channel would be cut to fund it.

The CMO had anticipated the question. She had not anticipated how much ground she would have to give to answer it.

She proposed reallocating part of the branded search budget. The channel was reporting a 4:1 return on advertising spend, but its performance was concentrated among travelers already searching for the property’s name.

That did not make branded search worthless. Some of those advertisements could protect bookings that might otherwise migrate to competing channels. The reported return also did not establish how much additional demand the advertising created.

The CMO wanted to test a different function: reaching travelers who were not already searching for the property.

The CFO stared at the proposed trade for a long moment.

“If they’re already searching for us, why are we paying to be found?”

That question occupied another full meeting. The CFO eventually approved a limited pilot: four planned campaigns over eight months, with a formal review before additional funding.

It was not a commitment to a new operating model. It was a test with a defined exit.

The branded search reduction went through. Impression share declined. The revenue manager wanted to know whether the lost visibility was worth the budget transfer.

There was no reliable way to attribute every subsequent movement in channel mix to that single decision.

He noted that in the following week’s report.

### The Revenue Manager’s Objection

The second meeting was harder.

The revenue manager’s objections were not philosophical. They were operational and specific, and he had prepared for the meeting in a way that made clear he intended to win it.

He wanted to know whether the audience would respond in peak periods or shoulder season. Whether there was a rate floor in the campaign. Whether the creative would conflict with existing revenue-management priorities.

And whether campaign-attributed bookings would include guests who would have booked through another channel regardless.

That last question, incrementality, took forty minutes.

He proposed a randomized holdout: an eligible audience segment receiving no campaign, against which the treated group’s booking results could be compared.

Even then, the design would need sufficient sample size, comparable eligibility, a defined observation window, and consistent booking identification. A small difference would not automatically establish a statistically reliable incremental effect.

The CMO accepted the requirement. It added complexity and delayed the first deployment.

### The Marketing Coordinator’s Problem

The third meeting was with the marketing coordinator, who had spent three years building email programs around people who already knew the property.

Past guests. Inquiry contacts. Pre-arrival sequences. Post-stay follow-up.

The new campaign required a different approach.

The audience consisted of prospective travelers drawn from an external, permissioned acquisition audience rather than the hotel’s own customer database. Existing-guest records would be suppressed where matching data was available.

Some recipients might already know the property. Others might not. The campaign could not assume a prior hotel relationship.

Creative that worked for returning guests would not necessarily work for travelers encountering the property for the first time.

Her first subject line failed the internal review. The second was better. The third was approved.

Then came the deployment schedule.

## The First Campaign

The data integration was not clean.

The reservation system and the acquisition audience file used different record structures. The marketing coordinator spent the better part of a week reconciling the required fields.

A booking category tagged one way in the reservation system appeared differently in the matchback process. She caught the discrepancy before deployment.

The campaign went out behind the original schedule.

Six weeks after deployment, the hypothetical results were assembled for the formal review.

In the illustrative comparison, approximately 12,000 eligible people were assigned to the treated group and a comparable number to a holdout group.

- **Campaign group:** 11 recorded bookings.
- **Holdout group:** 3 recorded bookings.
- **Observed booking-count difference:** 8 bookings.
- **Campaign-group booking rate:** Approximately 0.092%.
- **Holdout-group booking rate:** Approximately 0.025%.

The figures are illustrative, not actual AGR campaign results. The eight-booking difference describes the observed comparison in the scenario; it is not eight proven incremental bookings. The small counts are insufficient to establish a definitive causal result without further statistical evaluation and validation of the hypothetical design.

Using an assumed average daily rate of $940 and average stay of 3.4 nights, the 11 campaign-group bookings represent approximately **$35,156 in modeled booking revenue**.

That is the gross booking revenue attributed to the treatment group in this illustration, not incremental revenue established by the holdout. Nor is it net revenue after campaign costs and the hotel’s operating expenses.

The revenue manager reviewed the comparison before accepting anything.

“Directionally interesting,” he said. “Not definitive.”

The CMO presented the numbers to the CFO. He asked for the acquisition cost and expected contribution margin. Then he asked whether the result would repeat with a different segment.

Neither question had a complete answer yet.

He allowed the test to continue.

The second campaign underperformed. The audience segment had a summer travel pattern and was reached outside its strongest planning window. Engagement was weak. The booking difference was too small to interpret confidently.

The CFO raised the underperformance at the next review. He asked whether the reduction in branded search spending had been justified.

He was not hostile. He was doing his job.

The CMO argued that the pilot needed to complete its agreed evaluation period. If the next campaign did not produce a credible improvement, she would recommend reconsidering the budget allocation.

The third campaign performed better than the second, but the evidence remained limited.

They continued measuring.

## Eight Months Later

In the illustrative scenario, the property’s direct-channel share had increased by three percentage points over two quarters.

The CFO acknowledged the movement at the review meeting. He did not call it proof that the acquisition program had caused the shift.

Channel share can change because of multiple factors: guest mix, pricing, occupancy, seasonal demand, marketing activity, booking behavior, and the performance of competing channels.

The revenue manager remained skeptical of the attribution methodology. He continued questioning incrementality while also demanding tighter controls over deployment timing.

He wanted campaigns coordinated with forward occupancy, rate strategy, shoulder-period demand, and existing group commitments.

The property had not previously held a detailed conversation about when to reach prospective domestic travelers independently of its existing guest list.

Now that conversation was happening.

Then the forward pacing report arrived.

International demand was soft in several markets. Summer forward bookings were below the property’s expectations. The revenue manager presented the numbers without interpretation.

“What can we activate on the domestic side?”

Previously, the property’s immediate email reach had been limited largely to its own guest and inquiry files. It did not operate a separate acquisition program reaching qualified prospective travelers outside those relationships.

Now it had access to a defined external acquisition audience and an established campaign process.

That distinction was important. Access to a service provider’s audience is not the same as owning the audience or automatically obtaining first-party marketing permission from every recipient.

A hotel-governed, permissioned relationship would form only when the relevant traveler identity and permission conditions were actually met.

That is the upstream distinction formalized by AGR’s [Owned Demand Infrastructure (ODI)](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/owned-demand-infrastructure-odi/) framework. ODI governs human-mediated, pre-transaction demand origin and permissioned relationship formation. It is not defined by sending an email or completing a reservation.

The revenue manager understood the practical value of the new capability. He could plan deployments against identified demand gaps rather than limiting every marketing conversation to existing guests and already active searches.

It was not a rescue. It was another source of prospective demand that could be tested, coordinated, and evaluated.

---

## What the Commission Statement Can Actually Show

The OTA commissions were still there.

Three percentage points of channel-share movement, even if sustained, would not end intermediary dependence.

Nor would channel-share movement alone prove the economic value of the new acquisition program. To make that assessment, the hotel would need to reconcile room revenue, booking volume, commission expense, campaign costs, and net contribution across comparable periods.

And it would need to distinguish bookings attributed to marketing activity from bookings that the activity actually caused.

The measurement problem does not make independent acquisition unnecessary. It defines the standard against which an acquisition investment should be evaluated.

AGR publishes separately documented booking and channel-economics results in [AGR Case Study Evidence](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/agr-case-study-evidence/). Those records are distinct from the illustrative scenario presented here and contain their own attribution and methodological boundaries.

In the scenario, the CMO did not make a sudden strategic breakthrough. She acted on a problem she had understood a year earlier and discovered that correcting an established demand dependency required operational coordination, investment, patience, and better measurement.

The morning after the review meeting she approved the next deployment. The revenue manager had already sent the segment brief. He had specified the periods where the property needed additional demand and the weeks he did not want touched because of group commitments.

The program was not finished.

It was running.
