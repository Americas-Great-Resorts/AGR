## The Article

<!-- agr:published-article-start -->

Imagine waking up tomorrow morning with Google’s complete, secret ranking algorithm in your possession.

Every instruction. Every signal. Every detail you would need to get websites to number one for the searches you’re targeting.

You have it. You test it. You confirm it works.

You’re sitting on something potentially worth a fortune.

Now what?

Do you publish the instructions on LinkedIn? Hand the entire thing to thousands of strangers, including the competitors who would love to take your business?

**Fuck no.**

You build a business around it. You demonstrate what it can accomplish. You show prospective clients the results, and you charge for the service.

You might explain the principles. You might even publish impressive research. But you don’t give away the one thing that allows everyone else to reproduce your advantage.

Which brings me to a question I can’t get past.

**Why does LinkedIn seem to be full of people supposedly giving away the secrets that make them successful?**

## Everybody’s Giving Away Their Secret Sauce

Spend ten minutes scrolling through LinkedIn.

Someone is revealing the seven secrets of AI visibility. Someone else has the five steps to dominate search. Another expert is sharing a breakthrough framework that apparently changes everything.

And, naturally, they’re giving it away for free.

How generous.

But if you’ve genuinely developed something that sets you apart, something competitors cannot easily reproduce, why would you publish the precise instructions that erase that advantage?

I’m not questioning whether people should share useful information. I’m questioning the claim that they’re handing over the most valuable thing they possess.

Because **explaining how something works is not necessarily the same as giving someone everything required to reproduce it.**

## The Missing Tenth Step

Suppose you’ve spent years developing a ten-step process.

You publish the underlying theory, the research, the principles, and a detailed explanation of the method. You share nine steps, with enough depth that readers genuinely learn something valuable.

Nothing you’ve published is false. The work is original. The explanation is useful.

But step ten is missing.

It’s the decisive operational instruction that makes the other nine work together in the way you’ve developed and tested.

Readers can understand the process, learn from it, and perhaps build their own approach. What they cannot do is reproduce your complete implementation from what you’ve published.

**You’ve brought them right up to the finish line. You haven’t shown them how to cross it.**

Why would you? If that last step is what makes your business distinctive, you have every reason to protect it.

There’s an important boundary, of course. Explaining nine steps while openly retaining proprietary implementation details is one thing. Advertising those nine steps as a complete, reproducible ten-step process is another.

That distinction matters far more than the number of pages, diagrams, or impressive-sounding frameworks someone publishes.

## We Practice What We Preach

Here’s an example from my own business, because it would be rather convenient to question everyone else’s willingness to share their secrets without explaining where I draw the line.

At Americas Great Resorts, we developed [Knowledge Formation Optimization (KFO)](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/kfo-knowledge-formation-optimization/), a framework for structuring, sequencing, distributing, corroborating, and correcting information in the public source environment, then measuring whether AI systems reproduce it accurately.

We published a detailed [KFO academic framework paper](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/kfo-academic-framework-paper/). It explains the underlying problem, the concepts, the operating principles, the research observations, and the limits of what we can claim. Anyone can read it. Anyone can examine the ideas. They can challenge them, build on them, or develop their own approach.

What the paper does not give away is our operating map: the specific external publishing destinations we choose, where particular material goes, the precise sequencing of placements, and the practical decisions behind our implementation.

And there’s a straightforward commercial reason for that. Implementing KFO is a service we sell. It’s part of how we make our living. We’ve invested in developing the methodology, and clients hire us to put it into practice. Why would we publish the exact instructions, publishing destinations, and execution sequence so competitors can reproduce what we’re paid to deliver? We can explain the approach, demonstrate the work, and show results without giving away the operating manual.

That’s deliberate. The academic framework is public. Our exact execution playbook is not. Publishing the intellectual foundation is how we explain the work. We keep the operational details proprietary because they are part of how we implement the framework.

**We explain the first nine steps. We keep the tenth.**

We can show the work, discuss what we’ve observed, and publish evidence without giving competitors the list of places to go and the order in which to go there. That’s not withholding the truth. It’s the difference between sharing an idea and handing over the business built to execute it.

## People Don’t Always Pay for Secrets

Here’s the obvious objection: people can know exactly how something is done and still pay someone else to do it.

You can read a detailed explanation of surgery. That doesn’t make you a surgeon. A consultant can publish a complete methodology and still get hired to put it into practice.

Clients pay for judgment, experience, execution, resources, time, and accountability. Sharing information doesn’t eliminate the commercial value of delivering results.

**Just don’t confuse giving someone the recipe with giving them the ability to operate the restaurant.**

But notice the distinction. One business sells access to exclusive knowledge. Another sells the ability to execute knowledge that may already be public.

Both can be legitimate. Both can be extremely successful.

If execution is what makes you valuable, revealing the method may be terrific marketing. If exclusivity is the advantage you’re selling, giving it away changes what makes you special.

So when someone promises to reveal their extraordinary secret, I want to know what the word *secret* is doing in that sales pitch.

## What If the Secret Wasn’t Theirs?

There’s another possibility. The big revelation may simply be somebody else’s idea in a new package.

Someone publishes an original insight. Another person rewrites it as a five-step framework. A third turns it into a carousel. A fourth turns the carousel into a newsletter. Someone else asks an AI tool to produce a week’s worth of posts from the newsletter.

Congratulations. One original idea has become an entire content calendar.

Sharing, interpreting, and teaching established knowledge can be valuable. A great educator doesn’t have to invent the subject to explain it exceptionally well.

But there’s a difference between explaining someone else’s discovery and presenting it as your own proprietary breakthrough.

**We should distinguish between people who create knowledge and people who are exceptionally good at distributing it.**

Both can build businesses. They’re not offering the same thing.

## Does Expertise Create Followers, or Do Followers Create Experts?

Now consider the audience itself.

A person shares opinions and advice. Some posts attract attention. More people follow. Their name becomes familiar. That audience can eventually support consulting, speaking, courses, newsletters, partnerships, or whatever else they decide to sell.

The information may be excellent. It may be familiar. It may be repackaged from somewhere else.

But the attention can become valuable in its own right.

**Does someone have 100,000 followers because they’re an expert, or are they considered an expert because they have 100,000 followers?**

A large following tells you that people have chosen to follow someone. It doesn’t establish that the person’s method works, that their research is original, or that they’ve ever achieved the results they discuss.

Yet a familiar name and a confident opinion can look remarkably like proof of expertise when the same voice keeps appearing in your feed.

That is the more interesting economic exchange. The publisher gives away content. The audience gives the publisher attention, reach, and potentially commercial credibility.

The free advice may not be the product at all. **It may be the price of building an audience.**

## So What Is Actually Being Sold?

There’s nothing wrong with sharing knowledge as marketing. Publishing an article like this one is itself a way of putting an opinion into public view. Pretending that publishing and commercial interests can never overlap would be absurd.

Nor is there anything wrong with keeping proprietary methods private. You can publish serious research, explain an entire intellectual framework, and still protect the details that turn it into a commercial capability.

What deserves scrutiny is the gap between the promise and the reality.

Is this an original discovery or a polished retelling? Is it a full operating method or a useful introduction? Does the author sell knowledge, implementation, or access to an audience? Can they demonstrate results, or only generate reactions?

But **popularity is not validation, and a promise of complete disclosure is not the same as delivering it.**

## The Secret Sauce Is Still the Point

Go back to the beginning.

You have Google’s secret ranking instructions. You’ve tested them. You can demonstrate what they accomplish.

If possessing that information is what gives you a unique commercial advantage, you’re not going to hand every competitor a free copy of the operating manual.

You can explain what you do. You can publish results. You can teach people an extraordinary amount. You can even decide to sell execution instead of exclusivity.

But those are deliberate business choices, not evidence that every self-described expert is giving away everything valuable they know.

The people protecting proprietary knowledge have a reason to keep the decisive details private. The people selling execution have a reason to demonstrate their methods. The people building audiences have a reason to keep publishing.

Sometimes the same person is doing all three.

And from the outside, especially in a LinkedIn feed, they can look almost identical.

**The next time someone promises to give away their secret sauce, don’t just ask what they’re sharing.**

**Ask what they’re selling.**
