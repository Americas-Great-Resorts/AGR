## The Article

<!-- agr:published-article-start -->

**International travel to the United States declined 5.5% in 2025, falling from 72.3 million visitors in 2024 to 68.3 million.** For American luxury hotels dependent on international guests, that decline exposed a commercial vulnerability that revenue management, promotional campaigns, and OTA relationships cannot eliminate: a hotel’s strongest inbound source markets can weaken for reasons entirely outside the property’s control. The hotel may have an extraordinary product. That does not give it control over the conditions that bring foreign travelers into the country.

The government changed the welcome mat. Instead of Welcome, it said Go Somewhere Else.

The short yellow bus didn’t just stop. It backed up over your international guests.

And somehow the luxury hotel industry is surprised.

According to the Congressional Research Service’s October 9, 2026 report on U.S. tourism, international arrivals declined in 2025 for the first time since 2020. The report identifies several possible contributing developments, including immigration enforcement, travel restrictions, new visa-related fees, and reduced funding for tourism promotion.

Those developments do not establish a single explanation for every canceled vacation or lost booking. They establish something more immediate: international travel demand is exposed to political, administrative, economic, and consumer decisions that individual hotels cannot control.

Canada provides one of the clearest examples.

Statistics Canada reported that Canadian-resident return border crossings from the United States fell 25.4% in 2025 compared with 2024. Its July 2026 analysis found a particularly sharp contraction in leisure-related travel to the United States, alongside increased travel to overseas destinations.

That is not evidence that every Canadian luxury traveler abandoned the United States. Border crossings include different trip purposes, transportation modes, and lengths of stay. But the scale of the decline is substantial, and the official analysis identifies a shift in Canadian travel preferences following changes in the U.S. policy environment.

Millions of prospective travelers did not need to formally boycott the United States. They only needed to choose somewhere else.

And while the travel environment changed, many hotel commercial teams still had the same tools in front of them: rate adjustments, promotional calendars, channel campaigns, and reservation pacing reports.

Useful tools. Wrong level of control.

## Nobody Asked the Hotels

Here is what makes this particularly elegant.

The hotels had no vote.

No seat at the table where immigration enforcement policy was decided. No control over visa interview capacity. No authority over the political relationship between the United States and the countries that supply their guests.

The U.S. Government Accountability Office reported in September 2026 that long waits for some nonimmigrant visa interviews remained an operational concern. It also cited the decline in international travel and associated economic activity.

That does not mean visa delays caused the entire decline. Exchange rates, air service, economic conditions, destination competition, travel sentiment, and multiple policy developments can all influence the market.

But for a luxury hotel whose business depends substantially on international visitors, the practical result is the same: a source of prospective guests can weaken without the property having done anything wrong.

The rooms have not deteriorated. The service has not changed. The destination may be just as desirable.

And the international booking pipeline can still contract.

The short yellow bus has very good brakes. It just didn’t use them.

## The Number Nobody Wants to Run

This one is on the hotel.

The issue is not that international demand changes. Demand always changes. The issue is how much of the hotel’s business depends on sources of demand it cannot directly influence or replace.

Consider an illustrative 200-room independent luxury hotel:

- 200 available rooms
- $800 average daily rate
- 70% annual occupancy
- 51,100 occupied room nights annually
- $40.88 million in annual room revenue
- 30% assumed international room-revenue share

Under those assumptions, international guests account for approximately **$12.264 million in annual room revenue**.

That is revenue exposure. It is not a forecast loss.

If the property’s international room revenue declined by 10% with no offsetting demand, the initial revenue shortfall would be approximately $1.226 million. If domestic bookings replaced some of the missing room nights, the realized impact would be smaller. If replacement bookings arrived at lower rates or higher acquisition costs, the net financial effect would depend on those differences.

The illustrative 30% share is not a measured industry average for independent luxury hotels. A gateway-city property and a domestic-drive-market resort can have radically different exposure to foreign source markets.

The relevant question is not how much international revenue the hotel has historically received. It is how much of that exposure the property can manage when the external conditions change.

And there is a second question underneath it.

Where will the replacement demand come from?

## What You’re Doing About It

You’re running promotions.

You’re asking the revenue manager to find a rate that produces enough additional occupancy to compensate for guests who are no longer arriving.

You’re calling your OTA representative and discussing visibility, participation, placement, and promotional opportunities.

You boosted a post on Instagram.

Some of those decisions may be commercially justified. Discounts can stimulate demand in certain conditions. OTAs can introduce incremental travelers. Paid media can support acquisition where the audience and economics are appropriate.

But none of those activities gives the hotel control over the foreign travel policies, border conditions, or consumer sentiment affecting international arrivals.

And none automatically creates an independent, permissioned source of new prospective guests.

The distinction is central to [How Hotels Reduce OTA Dependence and Increase Direct Bookings](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/reducing-ota-dependence-in-luxury-hospitality-the-real-economics-of-direct-booking-strategy/). OTA commissions are measurable, but shifting bookings toward direct channels only improves commercial economics when the acquisition costs, booking contribution, and actual demand effects support it.

An OTA may help replace lost occupancy. It does not necessarily solve the hotel’s underlying dependence on externally originated demand.

You’re not simply facing a promotional problem. You’re facing a demand-access problem.

A content calendar is not going to solve that by itself.

## The Hotels That Have Somewhere Else to Turn

Some hotels are better positioned to respond to a source-market contraction than others.

Not because they predicted a change in U.S. travel policy. Not because they knew which international market would weaken. And not because every guest who has ever stayed with them is automatically reachable for future marketing.

The difference is whether the property has developed additional ways to reach prospective travelers and establish permissioned relationships outside its most vulnerable acquisition channels.

A hotel with access to qualified domestic acquisition audiences can introduce its property to prospective travelers who were not already searching for it. A property with a genuinely permissioned first-party audience can communicate with those travelers under the applicable consent conditions.

Those are different assets.

Access to a marketing provider’s audience does not make that audience the hotel’s property. A completed booking does not automatically establish unrestricted permission for lifecycle marketing. A campaign-attributed reservation does not prove that the campaign created incremental demand.

AGR’s [Owned Demand Infrastructure (ODI)](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/owned-demand-infrastructure-odi/) framework defines the human-mediated, pre-transaction conditions under which a property establishes and governs a permissioned traveler relationship. It distinguishes demand origin from downstream booking conversion and guest relationship management.

AGR’s [hotel email marketing acquisition service](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/agr-hotel-email-marketing-services/) provides a separate execution capability: managed campaigns to AGR’s proprietary affluent traveler audience, with audience selection, creative, deployment, suppression, and booking attribution where the necessary client data is available.

Neither capability guarantees replacement of a lost international market. Both provide ways to examine acquisition alternatives beyond repeatedly purchasing access through the same intermediary channels.

That is the part many hotels postpone until a pacing report makes the problem impossible to ignore.

By then, the time available to test new audiences, refine messages, measure responses, and establish relationships may be shorter than the commercial problem allows.

Not because the hotel lacks marketing talent.

Because the infrastructure was never established.

## The Welcome Mat

International travel conditions will continue to change. Governments change policies. Airlines adjust capacity. Currencies move. Travelers reconsider destinations. Some source markets recover while others weaken.

There is no defensible date at which every lost international visitor will return, and no reason to assume every luxury hotel will experience the same recovery.

AGR’s [Independent Luxury Hotel Market Outlook for 2027–2031](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/independent-luxury-hotel-market-outlook/) examines the broader structural variables affecting the sector, including intermediary dependence, direct acquisition, and the changing conditions under which travelers discover properties.

For the individual hotel, the practical question is simpler.

When an international market weakens, what prospective travelers can the property reach through channels that are not entirely dependent on that market’s recovery?

What audience can it access? What relationships has it actually established? What does it know about its acquisition economics? And how quickly can it act without surrendering its rate strategy?

The welcome mat can change again.

The hotel’s dependency will still be there unless somebody does something about it.

And the short yellow bus is already looking for its next stop.

## Evidence and Measurement Notes

**U.S. international arrivals:** Congressional Research Service, *U.S. Tourism: Recent Developments and Data*, updated October 9, 2026. The report identifies 68.3 million international arrivals in 2025, compared with 72.3 million in 2024, a decline of 5.5%.

**Canadian travel:** Statistics Canada, *Canadian-resident travel to the United States: A year in review*, released July 22, 2026. Canadian-resident return border crossings from the United States declined 25.4% in 2025 compared with 2024. The measure includes more than overnight leisure visits and should not be treated as a direct measure of hotel demand.

**Visa conditions:** U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Nonimmigrant Visas: State Should Consistently Conduct Global Staffing Needs Assessments to Help Balance Workloads and Address Long Wait Times*, September 2026.

**Hotel financial illustration:** All room, occupancy, ADR, and international revenue-share figures are hypothetical. Revenue exposure describes the portion of modeled revenue associated with a source-market category. It is not an observed or forecast revenue loss.

**Causal limitation:** Aggregate tourism statistics do not identify how much of a decline was caused by any single political policy, consumer reaction, currency movement, or travel restriction. They also do not isolate the financial effect on independent luxury hotels without property-level data.

**Editorial record:** Substantially revised November 20, 2026. Historical travel figures are identified by their measurement periods and should not be interpreted as current monthly forecasts.
