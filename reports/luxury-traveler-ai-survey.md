## Quick Answers

### Have surveyed travelers booked a hotel they first heard about from AI?

Among 2,768 respondents who selected a Q5 answer, 1,530 (55.3%) said they had done so in the past 12 months. Another 884 (31.9%) said no, and 354 (12.8%) were not sure. The 794 submissions that left Q5 blank are excluded from those Q5-answerer percentages. The finding reports remembered discovery and booking, not independently verified referrals or incremental bookings.

### Which AI assistant is most frequently selected for travel planning?

ChatGPT received 1,909 selections, equivalent to 53.6% of all submitted surveys. Google AI Mode or AI Overviews received 1,633 (45.8%) and Gemini 898 (25.2%). Q3 allowed multiple selections, so these are not exclusive market shares and cannot be summed into a unique-user count.

### Where do respondents say they check an AI hotel recommendation?

The hotel’s own website received 2,130 selections (59.8% of all submissions), followed by Google reviews with 1,833 (51.5%) and Tripadvisor with 1,057 (29.7%). Q6 allowed multiple selections and was optional. These totals describe selected verification sources, not actual observed visits or the sequence of checks.

### What do respondents trust for the final luxury hotel decision?

In Q8, the hotel’s own website received 1,224 selections (34.4% of all submissions), Google reviews 1,112 (31.2%) and friends or family 997 (28.0%). An AI assistant received 285 (8.0%). Respondents could select up to two sources. The data do not establish a single top source per individual or whether any specific respondent used AI earlier in the same booking.

### Would respondents allow AI to book and pay on their behalf?

Of the 2,719 who answered Q9, 1,631 (60.0%) chose ‘No.’ Another 680 (25.0%) would let AI hold a room but would pay themselves. The remaining 408 (15.0%) selected some willingness to let AI book and pay, either for any hotel or only one they had previously stayed at. These are stated preferences, not experiments in autonomous booking.

**Scope:** These findings describe voluntary submissions from one selected AGR email audience. They are not population-wide estimates, and aggregate results cannot link individuals across questions.

## At a glance

1.  **Reported discovery can precede booking.** Of those answering Q5, 55.3% said they had booked a hotel they first heard about from an AI assistant. This is respondent recall, not transaction-level attribution.
2.  **AI is selected more often for discovery than for making the booking.** Q4 recorded 1,959 selections for finding hotels to consider and 178 for making the booking. This does not establish a conversion funnel because the selections cannot be matched to individuals.
3.  **Hotel websites remain prominent in checking and deciding.** The hotel’s own website was selected by 2,130 respondents in Q6 and by 1,224 in Q8, two differently worded questions with different response limits.
4.  **Respondents report AI errors.** Wrong prices received 1,072 Q7 selections, and wrong amenities or restaurants received 908. These are unverified reports; overlapping selection is possible.
5.  **Payment authority remains a separate issue.** Among Q9 answerers, 60.0% would not let AI book and pay, while 25.0% would permit a hold but pay personally.

## What this study measures

The study records what participating travelers selected about their leisure hotel experience, AI assistants used, planning activities, self-reported AI-originated hotel bookings, verification behavior, inaccurate information, sources trusted for the final decision, willingness to delegate booking/payment, and the channel used for their most recent luxury hotel reservation.

**A selection is not necessarily a respondent-level trajectory.** The final spreadsheet preserves only aggregate counts by answer. It does not preserve which respondent selected which answers. Cross-question statements cannot claim that travelers using AI for hotel discovery were the same individuals who consulted hotel websites, trusted one source, or booked through any particular channel.

**None of the ten individual questions was mandatory; however, at least one response was required to submit the survey.** For five single-answer questions (Q1, Q2, Q5, Q9, Q10), we can calculate the number that supplied no response from the reported total of 3,562. For multiple-answer questions we cannot reconstruct the number of unique respondents who answered, because a person could select more than one option. In those questions, percentages are consistently calculated as selections divided by all 3,562 submissions. These are shares of the survey-submission cohort selecting an option, not question-answerer shares.

## What this means for luxury hotels

This section is interpretation, not another measured result. The survey suggests AI can play a meaningful role in introducing hotels, while hotel-owned information and established review sources continue to receive substantial selections for verification and the final decision. At the same time, respondents reported wrong AI information, and many are unwilling to delegate payment. None of these patterns establishes causality or which channel ultimately captured demand from an AI-originated discovery.

**Check the information a traveler can validate.** A hotel can examine whether its own website clearly communicates room categories, rates, amenities, restaurant availability, and reservation details. The survey identifies those categories as relevant to respondents; it does not test whether changing website content improves AI recommendations.

**Distinguish discovery from booking attribution.** An AI introduction is not proof of a direct booking, and a direct booking is not proof of AI influence. Hotels seeking to measure these relationships need consented, respondent- or session-level attribution methods not available in this survey.

**Do not mistake willingness for autonomous purchase adoption.** The Q9 preference responses and Q10 reported booking channels measure different constructs. Product adoption cannot be calculated by subtracting them.

**Monitor answer quality independently of traffic.** Wrong prices, amenities, and operational status were reported by participants; hotels should verify their public information and test representative AI responses, without assuming this survey measured actual error rates.

## Methodology

**Study design and instrument.** We designed a ten-question, self-administered multiple-choice survey. The respondent-facing title was “How you choose a luxury hotel now.” The instrument described the survey as taking under three minutes. Questions covered past-year leisure nights, typical leisure hotel nightly rate, AI assistants used for travel planning, AI planning activities, whether a hotel first heard about through AI was subsequently booked, verification sources, reported incorrect AI hotel information, up to two trusted final-decision sources, willingness to let AI book/pay, and channel of the most recent luxury hotel booking. The survey instrument and aggregate response spreadsheet form the supporting research materials.

**Recruitment and fieldwork.** On November 2, 2026, we emailed the survey to 525,000 selected records from our proprietary high-net-worth frequent luxury-traveler audience. Our [data strategy](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/integrated-data-strategy/) describes the travel-interest, demographic, and other criteria used in audience selection. The survey itself did not verify individual respondents’ income or net worth. We accepted responses through November 19, 2026, at 5 p.m. Eastern and compiled the results that day.

**Delivery and participation.** The email deployment achieved 96.2% deliverability and a 24.7% unique open rate. Applying the deliverability rate to 525,000 invitations yields an estimated 505,050 delivered messages. We received 3,562 survey submissions; each contained at least one answer because the form did not permit a completely blank submission. This equates to 0.68% of invitations sent or 0.71% of estimated delivered invitations. The retained campaign figures do not specify whether the unique open rate was calculated against sent or delivered messages, and survey-link click totals are unavailable. An email open does not independently establish that a person read the invitation. These rates describe this email deployment, not a probability-sample survey response rate.

**Answer design and denominator rules.** No individual question required a response, but at least one answer was needed to submit the form. Q1, Q2, Q5, Q9, and Q10 allowed one answer each. Q3, Q4, Q6, and Q7 allowed multiple selections. Q8 permitted up to two. For single-answer questions, exhibits use the number of recorded answers to that question as the primary denominator, and state the number missing. For multi-select questions, exhibits report each answer count divided by all 3,562 survey submissions, because unique question-answerer counts are not available. Multiple-answer percentages can exceed 100% when added. A blank response is not recoded as “No,” “None,” or “Not sure.” All percentages are recalculated from raw counts and rounded to one decimal place.

**Missing responses in single-answer questions.** Q1: 3,055 answered, 507 missing. Q2: 2,662 answered, 900 missing. Q5: 2,768 answered, 794 missing. Q9: 2,719 answered, 843 missing. Q10: 2,381 answered, 1,181 missing. These missing totals are calculated by subtracting answer selections from 3,562, assuming the platform allowed at most one selection per question as shown by the instrument.

**Data retention and reproducibility.** We retained aggregate answer counts rather than individual response records or combinations of answers. We reviewed the tabulated counts before publication. The exhibits are reproducible from the final aggregate counts and the denominators stated alongside them. Because respondent-level records were not retained, we cannot reconstruct individual answers or perform cross-tabulations, conditional analyses, or respondent-level attribution. We did not calculate survey weights, confidence intervals, or significance tests. The aggregate workbook alone cannot independently verify the original entries held by the survey platform.

**Sampling limitations.** This is a voluntary, nonprobability email survey of selected AGR records. The recruitment file is commercially relevant to luxury hospitality but is not a representative probability sample of all affluent travelers, all U.S. travelers, or luxury-hotel guests. Unknown nonresponse and coverage differences prevent national generalization or conventional sampling margins of error. Demographic selection attributes were not independently verified in the survey responses supplied. Self-reported past use, bookings, inaccuracies, and future willingness are subject to recall, interpretation, and self-selection effects.

**Question wording and construct limits.** “Used AI for making the booking” (Q4) does not specify whether AI executed payment. “Booked a hotel first heard about from an AI assistant” (Q5) does not identify the ultimate booking channel. “Where do you check” (Q6) is a stated behavior, not tracked visits. Q7 errors are respondents’ reports rather than verified inaccuracies. Q8 permits up to two choices and does not rank them. Q9 is hypothetical willingness. Q10 “Hotel website or phone” combines two direct booking routes, and Q10 concerns the most recent booking rather than Q5’s past-12-month discovery question. Questions concern different periods or conditions and cannot be pooled as a funnel.

**Potential instrument ambiguity.** “None,” “Did not use AI,” “No, never,” and “Don’t check” were offered alongside other responses within select-all questions, without individual-level data to verify logical exclusivity. Q3’s “None” and Q4’s “Did not use AI” are not equivalent constructs. These limitations do not invalidate the aggregate counts, but constrain interpretation.

**Data availability.** The research materials consist of the ten-question instrument, the aggregate response counts, and methodology notes. Respondent-level submissions were not retained in the research files; individual-level cross-question analysis and independent reconstruction of the original submissions are not possible.

## Prior and related work

This survey adds travelers’ self-reported use of AI to AGR’s earlier measurements of which luxury hotels AI systems recommend, how those recommendations change, and where the accompanying links lead. The studies examine different units of observation and were conducted under different protocols. Their counts are not pooled, and none establishes that an AI recommendation caused an individual booking.

**[The AGR Luxury Hotel AI Visibility Index](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-visibility-index/) (July 29, 2026).** Our six-market study recorded 824 ranked hotel recommendation slots in 180 AI answers. It examined how recommendations were distributed among luxury hotels, before any traveler decision or booking could be observed. The present survey addresses a different question: how participating travelers say they use AI when considering hotels.

**[Which Hotels Do AI Engines Recommend? A Study of 10 Destinations](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-recommendations-research/) (September 27, 2026).** This study assessed 1,177 substantive answers across ten destinations and four AI engines, recording 6,110 featured hotel appearances. It documented variation by engine and travel question. The traveler survey complements those measured recommendations with reported discovery, verification and booking behavior.

**[Does AI Recommend the Same Hotels Five Weeks Later?](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-recommendations-five-weeks-later/) (November 2, 2026).** Repeating the September question set showed that seven of ten destinations retained the same leading hotel while individual hotel lists changed. That is evidence about AI answer variation, not a measure of how travelers reacted to those recommendations.

**[The AGR AI Hotel Link Study: Where 900 AI Answers Send Travelers](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-link-study/) (November 11, 2026).** The link study recorded where citations and links in 900 AI hotel answers pointed. Among 899 answers naming a hotel, 536 contained no hotel or hotel-brand website link. The new survey separately records where participants say they check recommendations and where they report booking. No individual traveler path connects the two datasets.

**[Ask AI the Same Question in Three Languages. Get Three Different Answers.](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-hotel-recommendations-by-language/) (November 17, 2026).** This Miami study examined recommendations across English, Spanish and Portuguese questions and different country origins. It found variation even when questions were repeated under the same conditions. The survey does not independently measure how language or location affected respondents’ use of AI.

AGR’s [AI Visibility, KFO & Hospitality AI Resource Index](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/ai-visibility-resources/) organizes additional original research by subject. Our [data and targeting strategy](https://www.americasgreatresorts.net/integrated-data-strategy/) describes the travel-data audience used for survey recruitment. That audience description is sampling background, not evidence that voluntary respondents represent all luxury travelers.
